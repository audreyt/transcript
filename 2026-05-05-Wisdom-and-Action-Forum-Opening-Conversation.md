### Toby Walsh:
And so then let's wind forward to, I guess, when you did start to really make an impact politically. Tell us about the Sunflower Movement and your engagement in that.

### Audrey Tang:
So in 2014 Taiwan was facing a real polarization crisis, because as we know, at the time many people in social media — even though we follow the same people — the algorithm, predicting that we will get more engagement through enragement, starts pushing unsolicited information that amplified the outrage, not the overlap.

And Taiwanese people are already by that time one of the most connected countries in the world, and therefore the most polarized. The president was enjoying 9% approval rating in 2013. So in the country of 23 million, anything he says, 20 million people against him — but of those 20 million people were also very polarized. One side thinks we must be free from China; the other side thinks that we *are* the free China. And they were really at each other's throats. And so in March 2014 things came to a head when the parliament tried to rush through a trade agreement with Beijing that would open up our telecommunication, publication, cybersecurity sector to investments from Beijing. And of course we think it deserves a deliberation that we didn't get one. So I supported students to occupy the parliament peacefully for three weeks, and we say always that we're not protesting, we're demonstrating — we're showing that it is possible to get half a million people on the street and many more online to actually see each other and agree, instead of being consumed by the antisocial social media like wildfire, to strip away the oxygen and social fabric. We can actually sit around a campfire and have real conversation with people who think differently, in tables of ten. So after three weeks of peaceful occupation, we converged on a set of very coherent demand that the speaker of the parliament simply say, okay, the people's version are better. So let's go with it. So it became one of the very rare occupied movements that converged instead of diverged.

### Toby Walsh:

### Audrey Tang:

And using this kind of pro-social media, people can, despite differences, find their uncommon ground.

### Toby Walsh:

One is the normal chronological feed, and another is more like Community Notes, that allows for shared common knowledge around fact-checking and contextualization. ### Toby Walsh:

### Audrey Tang:

### Toby Walsh:

### Audrey Tang:

So we sent 200,000 text messages to random numbers around Taiwan from 111, the trusted official number, saying simply: tell us what should we do about this together. And a thousand people volunteered, and we chose 447 people as mini-public representatives of the Taiwanese public. And then again, table of ten, small campfire, with only one rule: if you think of an idea, if you can convince the other nine people at your table, your idea becomes the national agenda. ### Toby Walsh:

### Audrey Tang:
