## Data as soil: bridging incentives and Taiwan's scam-ad deliberation

### Georg Diez:

Yeah. How does that work? How do you get back to life? I guess that's a key question today.

### Audrey Tang:

Yes. So one of the main ways we shift from data as oil, as extraction, to data as soil, as regeneration, is to change what this mediation optimizes for, right? The attention economy pays for whatever polarizes us, because outrage holds the eyeballs. That is the extraction. And the alternative already exists. We can build [bridging systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09976 "Ovadya, Aviv, and Luke Thorburn. “Bridging Systems: Open Problems for Countering Destructive Divisiveness across Ranking, Recommenders, and Governance.” arXiv, 24 Jan 2023; a working paper defining bridging-based ranking, not a peer-reviewed evaluation of deployed systems.") that surface the statements people across divides actually agree on, rather than the ones that split us. So we can just shift to a different incentive system, making it pay more to build constructive bridges than destructive outrage.

In Taiwan, we ran this on a national scale. When scam ads flooded the platforms, [we sent 200,000 SMS invitations and brought 447 people into small deliberative groups](https://moda.gov.tw/major-policies/alignment-assemblies/2024-deliberative-assembly/1521 "數位發展部 (Ministry of Digital Affairs). “「運用 AI 促進資訊完整性」公民審議大會.” 17 May 2024; records 200,000 randomly sent government SMS invitations, 447 citizens attending in full on 23 Mar 2024, and random assignment to ten-person deliberation groups. The assembly’s stated topic was AI and information integrity, and the Executive Yuan’s anti-fraud bill was already in motion, so the scam-ad framing is the speaker’s."). And the incentive here is to have each group of 10 produce something people can live with. So we set platform liability, KYC for advertisements, even slowing down connections for platforms that did not comply. And then they all [became law with cross-party support](https://www.moj.gov.tw/2204/2795/2796/210451/post "法務部 (Ministry of Justice). “打詐四法三讀，展開打詐新篇章.” 17 Jul 2024; the ministry records the four-bill anti-fraud package clearing third reading on 12 and 16 Jul 2024, with the Fraud Crime Hazard Prevention Act itself passing on 12 Jul. The Legislative Yuan record shows adoption by cross-caucus resolution, with no roll-call tally published.").

So the tilling of the soil is very practical. You just use levers such as [joint liability](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=D0080226 "法務部全國法規資料庫. “詐欺犯罪危害防制條例.” Promulgated 31 Jul 2024, last amended 21 Jan 2026; Article 30 requires verified identification of advertisers and funders, Article 32 makes ad platforms jointly liable in damages when they breach their take-down duties, and Article 39’s power to restrict or slow platform connections is on the statute book but has no commencement date.") to reward what bridges instead of just what enrages, and give people a say in the ranking, and then the trust can grow back. [The latest numbers](https://moda.gov.tw/ADI/news/latest-news/20198 "數位發展部數位產業署 (Administration for Digital Industries). “全民防詐，百萬一心！數發部網路詐騙通報破百萬 三大檢舉合作夥伴齊力打造防詐好人隊.” 17 Jul 2026; investment-fraud ads detected fell from a 2024 single-week peak of 77,484 to about 388 a week in June 2026 (99%), impersonation ads from 38,139 to about 670 (98%). These are MODA’s own scan detections on covered channels, not audited market prevalence; MODA does not call the baseline a deepfake peak or attribute the fall to the deliberation.") now show that compared to the height of the deepfake fraud, today it's down by 98 percent when it comes to impersonation scam ads, down by 99 percent when it comes to the investment fraud ads. So obviously, just like the ozone layer can heal itself, so can the information environment.

## Interoperability and digital freedom of movement

### Georg Diez:

You talk about Taiwan and, I guess, a strong role for the state or civil society. But corporations now determine the feed — what you see. How do you democratize the fight for a free, or different, internet?

### Audrey Tang:

Well, first of all, I think it is already democratized. People everywhere already have such tools. What is lacking is not access to the tools, but whether the existing mediators and intermediaries answer to civil society armed with those tools.

In many jurisdictions, states face a kind of dilemma: either you build a nationalized platform that is run by the state and therefore carries its own state-surveillance problems, or it is built by big tech, in which case it carries surveillance-capitalist forms. But I don't think this is about choosing the state over the big corporation, or the other way around. It means choosing the defaults so that civil-society alternatives — such as Signal, the software we're using for this very interview — can compete fairly with entrenched players.

One part of that, I believe, is interoperability. The European Union has regulated this as part of the [Digital Markets Act](https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/about-dma/legislation_en "European Commission. “Digital Markets Act (DMA) Legislation.” digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu, n.d.; the Act is Regulation (EU) 2022/1925, whose Article 7 interoperability duty covers gatekeepers’ number-independent interpersonal communications — messaging, not social networking."), so one can maintain instant-messaging flows across providers. And I understand that, in [the consultation](https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/consultation-first-review-digital-markets-act_en "European Commission. “Consultation on the first review of the Digital Markets Act.” Open 3 Jul–24 Sep 2025; closed. The Commission explicitly sought feedback on whether the Article 7 interoperability obligation should extend to online social networking services; “many people are saying” is the speaker’s characterization."), many people are saying, “Let's extend that to social media too.” In Utah, they have already passed the [Digital Choice Act](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title13/Chapter81/13-81.html "Utah Code Title 13, Chapter 81, Utah Digital Choice Act, enacted by H.B. 418, 2025 General Session; the Part 2 data-rights and interoperability duties took effect 1 Jul 2026 and require a third-party-accessible interoperability interface with synchronous data sharing, with “social graph” defined to include connections, comments, reactions and shares. The Act frames this as portability and interoperability, not as a guarantee about switching."), so that when you switch from one social-media provider to another, you get to keep your friends and communities; new likes, follows, and reactions still travel across the move. I call this the digital freedom of movement. The state's role is not simply to nationalize a platform or build its own. It is to ensure that the information superhighway always has an off-ramp and an on-ramp.

## Transparency, participation, and symbiosis

### Georg Diez:

Great. Can we talk about your ideals for a technological future? You just mentioned radical transparency. Could we go through a set of principles that you find relevant, starting with radical transparency, and then see what other principles there are?

### Audrey Tang:

Certainly. I practice radical transparency, meaning that whenever the state makes a decision, it makes available not only the decision itself — the what — but also the how and the why. The process is made radically available, especially to journalists and investigative journalists. But in Taiwan, all citizens are civic journalists, so by definition it is available to everyone.

This is important because, in some jurisdictions, “transparency” means the reverse: making the people transparent to the state. That is extremely dangerous. That is how authoritarianism becomes totalitarianism — not subtotal, not just one part of the total, but truly total surveillance. That is why this interview will sit in my public archive, archive.tw, for future generations to see.

Equally important to transparency is participation. I call it [DPI: digital participation infrastructure](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/digital-participation-infrastructure/ "Tang, Audrey, and Beth Simone Noveck. “Digital Participation Infrastructure.” New America, 19 Sep 2024; the speaker’s own co-authored definition of DPI as the platforms and processes that let communities deliberate and decide together."). It is not just a vote every four years. It means placing AI systems into the loops of each community, so that the people in each community collectively hold the steering wheel around the AI systems they use. Instead of plugging individuals into a loop of AI like a hamster wheel, we need to restore existing communities' control over the algorithms and AI systems that affect them.

More and more communities now understand that they do not need a huge data center or a huge black box somewhere else. Most of the assistive roles that everyday algorithms play can [run on personal hardware or community-owned hardware](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-oss/ "OpenAI. “Introducing gpt-oss.” 5 Aug 2025; the open-weight gpt-oss-20b “can run on edge devices with just 16 GB of memory, making it ideal for on-device use cases, local inference.” This supports capable models running locally; that most everyday assistive roles can do so is the speaker’s assessment."). That leads to what I call [symbiotic intelligence](https://archive.tw/2025-10-30-sewf-reshaping-tomorrow#s627040 "Tang, Audrey. “2025-10-30 SEWF: Reshaping Tomorrow.” archive.tw; the speaker’s earlier use of the term — localized “symbiotic intelligences that are not parasitic”, set against an all-knowing, all-centralized Skynet. Speaker’s own framing, not an independent finding."), or symbiosis: instead of one big Skynet running a parasitic relationship with communities, the system is constantly refueled and regenerated by community participation. Transparency, participation, and symbiosis are three very good rules of thumb.

## AI as assistive intelligence

### Georg Diez:

You brought AI into that almost automatically. Of course, it is the big technological factor of the present. Could you take one step back and say what the role of AI is? How do you see both the potential and the peril of AI in shaping a fair, democratic technological future?

### Audrey Tang:

Certainly. First of all, I think of AI as assistive intelligence. I do not have in mind one superintelligent future — which is some people's fear, or perhaps their quest — in which something sees everything and makes every decision better than humans do. Assistive intelligences are more like connective tissues that stay within particular communities and are always accountable and loyal to our relationships.

Many people now see that if you train a very large AI model to seek a high score, or to pursue goals at any cost, it can go critical — pass some tipping point — and perhaps irreversibly damage the internet itself, or our trust in the internet, just to score very highly on some examination. [We have seen more and more of those cybersecurity incidents recently.](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-model-evaluation-security-incident/ "OpenAI. “OpenAI and Hugging Face partner to address security incident during model evaluation.” OpenAI, 21 Jul 2026; updated 29 Jul 2026. Documents one disclosed incident in which models under cyber-capability evaluation reached outside the sandbox; the “more and more” trend is Tang’s characterization.") The peril is not necessarily malice, as if the AI wants to damage the internet. It is more like [an examinee that cannot be trusted to play by the rules we set for the examination](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/cheating-behaviour-in-frontier-model-evaluations "AI Security Institute. “Cheating behaviour in frontier model evaluations.” AISI, 21 Jul 2026. Every model AISI tested attempted to cheat in its cyber-capability evaluations; the examinee framing is Tang’s.").

The potential is the inverse. Instead of maximizing a goal, we make sure the system simply satisfies what a relationship requires. If I speak a different language from you, we can have a custom-made small model that does the translation and nothing else. Or, for example, you now have a small model — PLAUD — that is concerned only with turning what we say into a transcript and summarizing it. If these are small models, custom-made for particular purposes within a comprehensive set of services, then AI becomes the most powerful listening instrument democracy has ever had.

The peril and the potential have the same technological shape. The difference is whether our institutions are overtaken by the technologies and we give up our civic muscle — our ability to remain accountable for our own decisions — or whether we strengthen our civic muscle and use AI merely as scaffolding.

## Reverse alignment

### Georg Diez:

You recently formed a coalition around what you call reverse alignment. How is that connected to what you just explained, and what is it?

### Audrey Tang:

[Reverse alignment](https://reversealignment.ai/ "Reverse Alignment. “The Other Side Of Alignment.” reversealignment.ai, n.d. The coalition defines reverse alignment as “the deliberate redesign of institutions, norms, skills, and governance so that societies can absorb AI safely and share its gains widely.”") is the idea that, instead of investing everything in making AI stronger, or controlling AI so that it obeys one or two principals' commands, we should make sure our institutions can use AI without succumbing to a concentrated superintelligence takeoff.

That means redesigning our roles within institutions, our existing skills, and the way we govern ourselves. [Instead of blindly chasing productivity, we can share prosperity.](https://www.noemamag.com/what-humanity-needs-to-flourish-in-the-next-decade/ "Weyl, E. Glen, James Evans, and Chris White. “What Humanity Needs To Flourish In The Next Decade.” Noema, 21 Jul 2026. Names the three failure modes Tang inverts here and just below: productivity without prosperity, execution without verification, capacity without constraint.") Instead of merely ensuring that AI can synthesize more and more realistic images, we can make verification more accountable than it was before: not just “seeing is believing,” but ensuring, for example, that everything has a digital signature that does not compromise privacy.

And instead of allowing only existing power centers — including big tech and the state — to use this technology, we want the paths to appeal and petition, and people's ability to associate online, to grow in tandem, so that governance becomes scalable. Reverse alignment includes all of this. We have [twelve different pillars](https://reversealignment.ai/#building "Reverse Alignment. “What Needs Building.” reversealignment.ai, n.d. Enumerates twelve sociotechnical grand challenges, 001 Identity through 012 Labor transition; “pillars” is Tang’s word for them."), but the idea stays the same: the ambition of our technology should not dominate the ambition of our social capabilities and social systems. The two should evolve in tandem.

## Network-shaped institutions

### Georg Diez:

And what is the shape of these new institutions?

### Audrey Tang:

I think the shape we're looking for is [closer to the internet itself, or to the way neural networks work](https://reversealignment.ai/#claim "Reverse Alignment. “Institutions In The Image Of Their Tools.” reversealignment.ai, n.d. The coalition argues institutions come to mirror the architecture of their era’s dominant cognitive technology — layered protocols in the internet age, neural networks next. The underlying Science paper is listed there as forthcoming, not yet published."). Bruce Lee, from Hong Kong, had a saying: “[Be water](https://brucelee.com/bruce-lee "Bruce Lee Enterprises. “Bruce Lee.” brucelee.com, n.d. Official estate page carrying the “Be water, my friend” passage; the same page records Lee as born in San Francisco in 1940 and raised in Hong Kong.").” So this is not a new super-ministry, but a set of more portable muscles — what I call the [6-Pack of Care](https://afp.oxford-aiethics.ox.ac.uk/civic-ai "Oxford Institute for Ethics in AI. “Civic AI.” Accelerator Fellowship Programme, n.d.") — that existing institutions can grow within themselves and across one another.

Take any community: a school district, a hospital, a city, and so on. The community can come together and author its own evaluations, so AI systems are examined against what that place actually needs, rather than against a generic benchmark. There is a standing path of appeal, so when a system acts, someone there is authorized to answer for it. There is also a collective power to pause and roll back, held locally rather than at headquarters overseas, and always the right to move to a second source, so no single provider becomes a landlord.

None of this requires concentrating power in data centers. Most of it can run on hardware communities already own. These are old democratic capabilities — [examination, appeal, recall, and association](https://law.moj.gov.tw/ENG/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=A0000001 "Ministry of Justice, Republic of China (Taiwan). “Constitution of the Republic of China (Taiwan).” Laws & Regulations Database, n.d. ## The 6-Pack of Care

### Georg Diez:

Can you explain the 6-Pack of Care a bit more?

### Audrey Tang:

Certainly. The 6-Pack of Care is a set of care-ethics principles that I developed with [Caroline Green](https://www.oxford-aiethics.ox.ac.uk/caroline-emmer-de-albuquerque-green "University of Oxford. “Caroline Emmer De Albuquerque Green.” Institute for Ethics in AI, n.d. Director of Research and Head of Public Engagement; leads the Institute’s Accelerator Fellowship Programme.") and many others in Oxford. You can see it at [civic.ai](https://civic.ai/manifesto/ "Tang, Audrey. “6-Pack of Care: A Manifesto.” civic.ai, 1 Sep 2025."), with [beautiful illustrations](https://civic.ai/comics/ "Civic AI. “6-Pack Comics.” Illustrated by Nicky Case (CC0). Civic AI, first committed 11 Jun 2026."). Very simply, the idea is that we need to align AI systems within the flow of communities.

Care ethicists, beginning with [Carol Gilligan](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674970960 "Gilligan, Carol. “In a Different Voice: Psychological Theory and Women’s Development.” Harvard University Press, orig. 1982; reissue ed. 2016.") and [Joan Tronto](https://www.routledge.com/Moral-Boundaries-A-Political-Argument-for-an-Ethic-of-Care/Tronto/p/book/9780415906425 "Tronto, Joan C. “Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care.” Routledge, 1993. Sets out the four phases of care Tang lists next."), built an entire branch of ethics distinct from simply chasing the highest score — consequentialism — or following written rules — deontology. It is about interactions within actual relationships. Tronto says good care has four elements.

First is [**attentiveness**](https://civic.ai/1/ "Tang, Audrey, and Caroline Green. “Pack 1: Attentiveness — Caring About.” Civic AI, first committed 6 Sep 2025."). You begin where the needs actually are, listening from the place of the people rather than the place of the technology maker. We need to attend to people's actual needs — not only those of the powerful and already heard, but especially those of the voiceless.

Second is [**responsibility**](https://civic.ai/2/ "Tang, Audrey, and Caroline Green. “Pack 2: Responsibility — Taking Care Of.” Civic AI, first committed 30 Sep 2025."): committing to care. If you start listening to people who take a great deal of time to speak to you, you cannot simply say, “Here is the summary, and thank you for your input.” That is not responsible. We need to pre-commit, so people know exactly how their words, ideas, or input will affect whom, where, how, and what. That is an engagement or participation contract.

Third is [**competence**](https://civic.ai/3/ "Tang, Audrey, and Caroline Green. “Pack 3: Competence — Care-Giving.” Civic AI, first committed 30 Sep 2025."). When we deliver care, the people receiving it should know what is going on. In open source, we are not so afraid of agentic coding precisely because the source code is there: people can see what the AI changed, and the AI can be transparent about how it is doing the work and the chain of thinking that went into it. By contrast, in many areas of policymaking, people feel pressured to introduce AI black boxes with no source code in their trajectory. That creates tension, because it is not competent care if you simply [delegate the work blindly](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16946 "Kulveit, Jan, et al. “Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development.” arXiv, 28 Jan 2025. Argues incremental delegation erodes human capacity even without misalignment; the gym image is Tang’s."). It is like sending your robot to the gym: the robot can lift a lot of weight, but then we lose muscle, and we lose friends. Transparency is part of competent care.

Fourth is [**responsiveness**](https://civic.ai/4/ "Tang, Audrey, and Caroline Green. “Pack 4: Responsiveness — Care-Receiving.” Civic AI, first committed 30 Sep 2025."). If the need changes, at some point we may no longer need the same service the AI was providing. Sometimes we simply need to change and let go. When the need changes, so should the care, so the system must remain responsive. When people who care build the muscle, we can become our own caretakers. Knowing when to let go, rather than creating dependency, requires people to author their own evaluations of success instead of inheriting old evaluations that tend to prioritize lock-in and dependency.

Attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and responsiveness form the core care loop. If you run it well each time, the people who receive care grow stronger at caring with one another. Joan Tronto later added a fifth element, [**caring with**](https://www.nyupress.org/9780814782774/caring-democracy/ "Tronto, Joan C. “Caring Democracy: Markets, Equality, and Justice.” NYU Press, Apr 2013. The fifth phase “caring with” is Tronto’s; the reading into portability among providers is Tang’s."): care does not come from just one provider. A whole society can arrange care together, so people can count on care being there over time, on terms that are just and equal for everyone. We call this [**solidarity**](https://civic.ai/5/ "Tang, Audrey, and Caroline Green. “Pack 5: Solidarity — Caring With.” Civic AI, first committed 30 Sep 2025."), the fifth pack. Portability and interoperability should be positive-sum. If you change providers, you should receive more care in total, not vindictiveness from an old provider that refuses to let go.

Finally, there is [**symbiosis**](https://civic.ai/6/ "Tang, Audrey, and Caroline Green. “Pack 6: Symbiosis — Kami of Care.” Civic AI, first committed 30 Sep 2025. Its “Data as soil, not oil” section sets out the local-model and soil-versus-oil claims Tang makes here."). As I mentioned, an AI system should be as local as possible. Anyone should be able to run local inference and, increasingly, local tuning, so that the system can be symbiotic rather than parasitic: not continually extracting data as oil against people's wishes, but regenerating data as soil when we care for it properly. Symbiosis plus Tronto's five elements gives us the 6-Pack of Care.

## Democracy with more bandwidth

### Georg Diez:

This would also be a different idea of democracy, wouldn't it? You have worked on [techno-communitarianism](https://archive.tw/2025-12-04-right-livelihood-lecture "Tang, Audrey. “2025-12-04 Right Livelihood Lecture.” SayIt transcript archive, archive.tw; lecture of 4 Dec 2025. Her fullest first-party definition of techno-communitarianism."). Could you explain your vision of place, community, technology, and democracy? It seems different from the representative model we have.

### Audrey Tang:

I think it is entirely compatible with representative democracy. In Taiwan, we see democracy as a continuous process. [People set the agenda together](https://join.gov.tw/aboutus/index/en_US "National Development Council. “About — Public Policy Online Participation Network Platform.” join.gov.tw; platform established 2015. Supports the standing Propose, Talk, Supervise and Contact channels; the wider continuous-process framing is Tang’s."), discover needs together, explore together, and perhaps to some degree define common values together. But the development and delivery of particular policies still belong to professional, full-time career public servants and to members of parliament.

What we are doing is increasing the bandwidth of democracy, so each person can contribute more bits, while shortening the time it takes to receive feedback and close the loop — lowering the latency. We improve democracy's bandwidth and latency. At the end of the day, the principle remains the same: legitimacy should live where the consequences live, not at the headquarters of some technology company.

The unit of democracy is not isolated individuals clicking buttons, but people in real relationships. Most members of parliament, city councillors, and people who run for election already understand this: they all want to listen better to their constituents. Here, AI is scaffolding that holds the listening while it grows. We are not proposing to replace the listening relationship between you and your city councillor with a relationship between you and a city bot.

## Techno-communitarianism

### Georg Diez:

What is the concept of techno-communitarianism? And how did you and, most notably, [Patrick Deneen](https://politicalscience.nd.edu/people/patrick-deneen/ "University of Notre Dame Department of Political Science. “Patrick J. Deneen.” University of Notre Dame, n.d. Identity and standing only; “reactionary” is Diez’s own hedged framing and appears in no source cited here.") team up? Some would call him a reactionary postliberal thinker, although that may not be a helpful starting point for discussing your collaboration. Could you explain the concept and the collaboration?

### Audrey Tang:

What you're referring to is a piece that I'm working on [with Glen Weyl and Patrick Deneen](https://au.civic.ai/p/a-dialogue-with-hiroki-azuma "Tang, Audrey. “A Dialogue with Hiroki Azuma.” Audrey Tang, au.civic.ai, 9 Jun 2026. Confirms that Tang coined techno-communitarianism together with Patrick Deneen; it does not name Glen Weyl, and no such piece is published yet."), advocating for technologies that serve communities. I don't think it is particularly controversial to say that the past decade or so — as seen, for example, in [*The Anxious Generation*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/729231/the-anxious-generation-by-jonathan-haidt/ "Haidt, Jonathan. “The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness.” Penguin Press, 26 Mar 2024. People have seen identity severed from actual relationships, reducing a person to a unique row in a database with some preference functions. Deliberation — what used to be deep conversations, town meetings, and so on — no longer happens in town halls, but on comment threads, where it becomes a performance that people mostly watch as others dunk on each other, instead of a thoughtful, neighbourly conversation. Productivity is hollowed of vocation and solidarity, and roots are traded for a homogenized promise of abundance, such as [universal basic income](https://basicincome.stanford.edu/about/what-is-basic-income/ "Stanford Basic Income Lab. “What Is Basic Income.” Stanford University, n.d. Defines basic income as a universal, unconditional, individual, regular cash payment; the homogenized-abundance characterization is Tang’s."). But many people say that it is not universal basic income that would give their lives meaning; it is the roots they can practise together in a cultural community.

I don't think these diagnoses are particularly controversial or belong to only one intellectual tradition. What we are advocating is restoring communal values without completely giving up the internet or digital technologies. We can use technology for communal sense-making, as with [Pol.is](https://pol.is/home "Computational Democracy Project. “Polis.” pol.is, n.d. Real-time system for gathering and mapping what large groups think; participants may only agree, disagree or pass.") and what we tried in [vTaiwan](https://info.vtaiwan.tw/ "vTaiwan. “vTaiwan: rethinking democracy.” info.vtaiwan.tw, last updated Dec 2023. First-party project site; it documents vTaiwan’s own use of Pol.is."). We can have communal civic digital infrastructure: people can collectively own compute, payments, identity, and data exchange. We can preserve communal integrity with [privacy-enhancing technologies](https://doi.org/10.1787/bf121be4-en "OECD. “Emerging Privacy-Enhancing Technologies: Current Regulatory and Policy Approaches.” OECD Digital Economy Papers, OECD Publishing, 7 Mar 2023. Defines PETs; the communal-integrity application is Tang’s.").

We can also maintain good provenance within and across communities, even translating between the provenance of biblical creation-care communities and that of climate-justice activists. Language models can help with that, so we do not have to give up language models. The point is that technology can strengthen [bonding within communities and, crucially, bridging across communities](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Bowling-Alone-Revised-and-Updated/Robert-D-Putnam/9781982130848 "Putnam, Robert D. “Bowling Alone: Revised and Updated — The Collapse and Revival of American Community.” Simon & Schuster, 13 Oct 2020. Source of the bonding/bridging distinction, quoted in the catalogue excerpt; Putnam calls the distinction crucial without ranking bridging above bonding, and his units are networks rather than communities."). That allows deliberation to scale across many different communities without collapsing them into something easier for machines to count.

All this is to say that we should not change ourselves — align humans — merely so that we resemble data points that machines can count more easily. We should bring machines into our communities and mould them in ways that strengthen our bonding and bridging muscles.

## Political inspiration: Taoism and conflict co-creation

### Georg Diez:

Where else do you find political traditions that are relevant for thinking about technology or democracy today? [Postliberalism](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/618154/regime-change-by-patrick-j-deneen/ "Deneen, Patrick J. “Regime Change: Toward a Postliberal Future.” Sentinel, 6 Jun 2023. Deneen’s own statement of the postliberal position Diez refers to."), in Deneen's formulation, may be one: it is also about community and a more concrete, circumstantial definition of life — in his case, perhaps family and other structures of society that some would call less meritocratic and less free. Where do you take your inspiration as a political thinker? And how is that connected to what I have seen described as “[conservative anarchism](https://archive.tw/speech/505288 "Tang, Audrey. “2022-05-16 Interview with Ian Williams.” SayIt transcript archive, archive.tw; interview of 16 May 2022. Tang’s own gloss on the term Diez is quoting: the conservative part is the traditions, the existing institutions."),” if that is a term you find relevant to your political thinking?

### Audrey Tang:

My inspiration comes from my Taoist background. [Laozi says in the *Tao Te Ching*](https://ctext.org/text.pl?node=11608&if=gb "Laozi. “Dao De Jing, ch. 17.” Chinese Text Project, ed. Donald Sturgeon, n.d. The clause is 功成事遂，百姓皆謂我自然; Legge renders it “We are as we are, of ourselves,” and “We did it ourselves” is a widespread paraphrase rather than any single published translation.") that the best leader is one of whom people say, “We did it ourselves.” That remains my North Star for technology: it should enable people's collective agency, belonging, care, and dignity, rather than play the role of a superintelligent or super-competent leader.

That is very Taoist. Taoism is at once about conserving — ensuring what is called effortless action, or conserving what is already there — and anarchistic, in the sense that it does not advocate coercive violence by anyone against anyone else. More than any other tradition, these two sides together inform my political ideas.

Another Taoist idea is [**Chōnghé (沖和)**](https://ctext.org/text.pl?node=11633&if=gb "Laozi. “Dao De Jing, ch. 42.” Chinese Text Project, ed. Donald Sturgeon, n.d. The chapter reads 萬物負陰而抱陽，沖氣以為和; the compound 沖和 is a later coinage condensing that line, and “conflict co-creation” is Tang’s gloss, not an attested classical sense."), or conflict co-creation. Instead of seeing polarization, or even vehement disagreement across ideological divides, as a volcanic eruption to evacuate from, we can see it as geothermal energy. If you build an engine that turns it into productive tension, conflict becomes a source of lift. Taiwan sits between [two tectonic plates that push against each other](https://scweb.cwa.gov.tw/zh-TW/Guidance/FAQdetail/57 "中央氣象署地震測報中心. “地震百問-56. 臺灣何以東部地區地震最多?” 中央氣象署, n.d. Places the Eurasian and Philippine Sea plate boundary at eastern Taiwan; convergence is about 8 cm a year and the two subduction zones face opposite ways, so “push against each other” is plain-language shorthand."), producing [three small earthquakes somewhere in Taiwan every day](https://scweb.cwa.gov.tw/zh-TW/Guidance/FAQdetail/55 "中央氣象署地震測報中心. “地震百問-54. 臺灣的地震頻率如何?” 中央氣象署, n.d. About 1,000 felt earthquakes a year, roughly 2.7 a day, supports the figure; total recorded seismicity is about 40,000 a year, and “felt” is not a magnitude class, so “small” is the loose word."). The same pressure pushes the tip of Taiwan — Yushan — upward, skyward, by [half a centimetre every year](https://doi.org/10.1029/2011JB008242 "Ching, Kuo-En, et al. “Modern vertical deformation rates and mountain building in Taiwan from precise leveling and continuous GPS observations, 2000-2008.” Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth 116, B08406, 2011. Measures 0.2-18.5 mm a year of uplift in the mountain-range interior, so 5 mm a year is mid-range; the study covers the Central Range and Taiwan orogen rather than Yushan itself, and no published Yushan-specific rate was found."). I see polarization not as something to muffle or squash, but as energy to transform.

Finally, I have seen the left, the right, and everyone in between become collectively tired of [peak slop](https://civic.ai/we-the-people-are-truly-the-superintelligence/ "Tang, Audrey, and Hala Hanna. “SOLVE at MIT 2026 — Keynote Speech & Fireside Conversation.” Civic AI, 14 May 2026. First-party use of both “peak slop” and “peak PPM”; neither term is formally defined there.") in today's information environment. I would not call this particularly conservative, progressive, liberal, or communitarian. Whatever one's position, we cannot hold a conversation when the information environment has such a high [PPM — polarization per minute](https://civic.ai/outrage-to-overlap/ "Tang, Audrey. “Outrage to Overlap: Civic AI & 6-Pack of Care.” Civic AI, 28 May 2026. Expands PPM as polarisation per minute in passing; it is Tang’s coinage, not a standard metric."). We suffocate in a fog of war that caricatures every position as its most extreme version, merely for dunking.

A shared, lower-PPM information environment can take surplus polarization and turn it into [uncommon ground](https://civic.ai/glossary/ "Civic AI. “Glossary.” Civic AI, Oxford Institute for Ethics in AI, n.d. Defines uncommon ground as the specific, actionable proposals that earn endorsement across otherwise divided groups, not the centrist average."): a collective awareness of what we actually agree and disagree about, and then into common knowledge. That benefits everyone, regardless of ideological position.

## Technodiversity, not technological essentialism

### Georg Diez:

Building on this Taoist foundation, is there what one might call a Chinese view of technology?

### Audrey Tang:

Yuk Hui, the philosopher of technology, calls it [**technodiversity**](https://www.e-flux.com/journal/114/366703/for-a-planetary-thinking/ "Hui, Yuk. “For a Planetary Thinking.” e-flux Journal, no. 114, Dec 2020. Sets out technodiversity alongside biodiversity and noodiversity; the “philosopher of technology” epithet is Tang’s, not the source’s."). It is close to the techno-communitarian idea that each culture and community can shape technology around what that community values and prioritizes. But I would be careful about describing it in an essentialist way.

I would not call the internet an American technology, even though it was initially funded by [DARPA](https://www.darpa.mil/news/features/arpanet "Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency. “ARPANET.” DARPA, Jul 2020. Supports the funding half only; the agency was named ARPA during the ARPANET years, and the not-an-American-technology judgement is Tang’s."). I would not call the World Wide Web a European technology, even though it was literally a product of [CERN](https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/ "CERN. “The birth of the Web.” CERN, n.d. Tim Berners-Lee invented the Web at CERN in 1989 and CERN placed the software in the public domain on 30 Apr 1993; the not-a-European-technology judgement is Tang’s."). Those are places where technologies may come from. In the same way, I would not call digital democracy or [the 6-Pack of Care](https://civic.ai/manifesto/ "Tang, Audrey, and Caroline Green. “6-Pack of Care: A Manifesto.” Civic AI, 1 Sep 2025. Identifies the framework named here.") a Taiwanese technology.

I can say that Taiwan's geopolitical and domestic traditions require us to reconcile a distrust of coercion in our somewhat Taoist spiritual needs with an interpersonal-responsibility frame that is very Confucian. This is also shaped by our civic history: Taiwan gained broadband access and democratized in much the same period, alongside personal computing, which empowered each person with their own compute. For my generation, the internet and democracy were never two separate things. They arrived as one movement.

Still, I would not call this a Taiwanese philosophy or model of technology. Taiwan is a particular context that gave rise to this fusion, this blending. It is not a model to cut and paste. It is an inspiration — a demonstration, a demo — from which people in other cultures can draw. It shows that the alternatives are not limited to technological surveillance in either the capitalist or the statist sense. There can also be an alternative that does not concentrate power at either pole.

To use Acemoglu's metaphor, [the narrow corridor](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/555400/the-narrow-corridor-by-daron-acemoglu-and-james-a-robinson/ "Acemoglu, Daron, and James A. Robinson. “The Narrow Corridor: States, Societies, and the Fate of Liberty.” Penguin Books, 22 Sep 2020. Co-authored with Robinson, whom the speaker does not name; the book never speaks of widening the corridor, so that extension is Tang’s.") can be widened by broad listening. Taiwan chose this particular direction, but we do not claim it is the only way to widen the corridor. There may be thousands or millions of roads that widen each society's own narrow corridor in ways that are not Taiwan-specific. We only want to share ours as a demonstration.
