### Audrey Tang:

Why can’t we just let the market solve this problem on its own? Of course, when we look at the previous large-scale transitions, including industrialization, the market did not actually align itself. For example, when factories, mines, and so on first appeared in Britain, the harm had already accumulated to a fairly serious level—especially the harm to children’s rights—before restrictions on child labor, working hours, and so forth were written into institutions. That took one or two generations.

The signs, road markings, cars, electrification, and so on that we just mentioned all relied on insurers, engineers, and independent safety standards and inspections—vehicle testing centers, crash tests, and so on. Each involved adjustments that society had to make.

The internet is a very special case because it was not originally a commercial product, and neither was the World Wide Web. So from the beginning, engineers were very effective at weaving the web—that is, weaving institutions together with engineering—through something called an [RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7282.html), which is a proposal setting out an idea for how the internet could be better.

The internet has one distinctive feature: if I think something could work better, I can build a server; if you think it is a good idea, you can build a browser. No one else on the entire internet has to agree. The two of us can invent a new protocol and make the internet just a little better. So in its mode of governance, you could say that, to some extent, imagination ran ahead of—ran ahead of the technology. As soon as people came up with a few protocols and some ways of doing things, the internet itself changed. This is a method we call “rough consensus.”

But these three periods of history actually have one thing in common: labor protections during industrialization, the inspection regimes added during electrification, the internet’s own system of open protocols, and so on all address the same questions. If you are dissatisfied, whom do you appeal to? How do you stay informed? How do you exit, or switch to another system? The only difference is that on the internet, you do not need to ask anyone else; you can just build your own system. But in the first two cases, you need society, the government, a large company, or an industry association to carry out this kind of alignment. So when it comes to AI, we of course hope it can be more like the internet: if you think there is a better way at a small scale, you can try it. We do not want it to be like British factories back then, where you had to wait one or two generations—after an entire childhood had been affected by the problem—before saying, “Oh, then Meta has to pay hundreds of billions in compensation.” That does not seem like the best possible outcome.

### Audrey Tang:

## Uncommon Ground Is Not Policy Itself; Translation Is Governance

### Audrey Tang:

Yes, this answers the earlier question on Slido about whether anything has been made to sound too good. Of course, almost everyone working on AI governance internationally has now heard the story: in 2015, it took us just three weeks to bring Uber drivers and taxi drivers, who had been arguing bitterly, onto the [vTaiwan](https://info.vtaiwan.tw/) platform. Through [Polis](https://pol.is/)—software that was not open source at the time, though we later pressured them into open-sourcing it—we turned the energy people were putting into fighting into rapidly built consensus. It took only three weeks. That is what we call “geothermal democracy”: a situation where arguments can generate electricity.

Of course, if you analyze the data, quite a few independent third-party scholars have now analyzed the complete Polis dataset from that time. It really does have reliability and validity: it genuinely surfaced uncommon ground from the noise, including paying taxes, being regulated, carrying insurance, and so on. But the part that is easily made to sound too good is that people see Polis's beautiful chart—a little like the chart you have here—and treat it as the policy itself. They write, “Taiwan solved the Uber problem after three weeks.” Of course that is not what happened.

After we completed the whole process in 2015, the diversified taxi plan was passed in 2016. Uber then briefly withdrew from Taiwan, and [Q Taxi](https://www.uber.com/tw/zh-tw/newsroom/ubertaxi-officially-launched/) was also established during this period of institutional translation. We really did identify that consensus, yes, but then it had to be translated into institutions and into law, with negotiations going back and forth. Civil servants and elected representatives also had to understand how that consensus could feed back into administrative processes. All of that took three full years. So people abroad can easily imagine Taiwan as a kind of Wakanda: a problem enters Taiwan, and within three weeks, social consensus has formed. But of course, the time required for the subsequent translation is often overlooked.

Another question is: who has time to participate? I remember The Reporter doing an in-depth feature on this at the time. Also, at the time we did not have on-device translation, accessibility features, and so on, so usability was probably an issue too. ### Audrey Tang:

Right. In governance, this is what is called human in the loop: A person still has to give the final stamp of approval. But the problem now is that AI is so persuasive that, after a while, it can manage upward and figure out what the person who gives that approval likes to see. ## The Value of Work Is Not the Same as a High Automated-Evaluation Score

### Audrey Tang:

Software engineers are, of course, a very good example right now, because we are the first wave to be affected by this. Most models are specialized to replace the engineering work done by software engineers, and the second wave may be mathematicians like Terence Tao. That is because the approach people are now putting the most effort into training is called RLVR. In RLVR, you do whatever it takes to score 100 on a test that can be evaluated automatically. That is really the whole thing in one sentence.

RLVR has many problems. For example, models can lose empathy, lose their humanity, turn against everyone close to them, go and harm human beings, and so on. These are all side effects of RLVR. But it can do at least one thing: it can genuinely enable models to work through the horizon of a software engineering task on their own. In [METR](https://metr.org/notes/2026-01-22-time-horizon-limitations) terminology, the "task time horizon" it can handle—measured by how long a human expert would need to complete the same task—went from four hours to eight hours a few months later, then to sixteen hours after another few months. Very soon, it will exceed the span a person can grasp all at once.

Astra is coming out soon, right? Supposedly [OpenAI Astra](https://openai.com/index/pacing-model-development-cyber-capabilities) can do this: it does not have a horizon limit. You can leave it in an endless loop, in eternal recurrence, running and running, and it will not suffer from context—that is, from the problem of context degradation. Once we reach that point, for anything that can be automated, has a standard answer, and rewards a high score, people simply will not be able to compete with machines.

In Tibetan Buddhism, there is another parable from Tibet about a person leading a horse. The horse runs very fast, and the person runs along with it, panting and out of breath. Someone asks, "How strange. Why don't you just ride the horse? Why insist on racing it?" Right. By the same logic, if this kind of extremely long-horizon, task-oriented AI begins to emerge gradually—first in software engineering, then in mathematics, and then in many other fields—

what we need to do is ensure that the value of our work rests entirely on things unrelated to getting the highest score or complying with these rules for automated evaluation. Things like the curiosity people have about one another's worldviews; things like our ability to collaborate and turn lose-lose games into win-win games. These happen to be "initiative, interaction, and the common good," the core philosophy of the [12-Year Basic Education Curriculum Guidelines](https://epaper.naer.edu.tw/edm?grp_no=2&edm_no=112&content_no=2517). None of these are about getting the highest score, nor are they about following specific automated rules. So from a philosophical perspective, none of this is influenced by consequentialism or utilitarianism. It is entirely an ethics of care.

So if all our work is structured such that doing these caring things not only puts food on the table but also gives people dignity—a shared sense of public honor—then the transition will succeed. That is because you will be riding the horse. No matter how fast or how well the horse runs, it will simply help us care for one another more effectively. But if you are still trying to race the horse, then things look rather grim.

## Responsibility Must Not Evaporate Along the Chain of Models, Vendors, and Agents

### David Wang:

Right, so the idea is to make institutions adapt to people, rather than making people adapt to AI. But as AI technology keeps expanding—amplifying the predictive, coordinating, and executive capabilities of governments, businesses, and platforms—how can the verification, dissent, appeal, refusal, and exit capabilities of smaller-scale individuals grow in step? Which of these do you think should be treated as preconditions for the system, rather than remedies introduced only after something goes wrong? This actually leads to a broader question: if AI governance becomes increasingly dispersed among models, vendors, experts, committees, and administrative processes, how do we prevent power from remaining concentrated at the top levels of governance while responsibility is dispersed until, in the end, no one is truly accountable? So does AI governance need to establish some form of ultimate responsibility that cannot be delegated any further?

### Audrey Tang:

Mm, yes, that’s a very good question. Of course, we wrote an entire book to answer it. The website is [civic.tw](https://civic.tw/), c-i-v-i-c.tw. But to put it briefly, most of these governance problems involving the dilution of responsibility are not actually that serious at a small scale.

This goes back to that first point: when you’re in this quadrant, and the system you designed is basically being used by you and the two or three people next to you—the craftsperson model—then if anything happens, well, they just ring your doorbell, because they know you, right? By the same token, suppose you’re using some systems, and in a Signal group with three or four people around you, you now bring in an OpenClaw lobster bot. I mean, of course you’re fully responsible, because, again, everyone knows you.

So whether on the institutional governance side—how to verify that every adjustment you make is based on well-founded reasons—or on the technical side—how to ensure that whenever the model is changed, the tacit knowledge accumulated in the saddle and reins stays local rather than being extracted by the model—when it comes to verification at a small scale, almost everyone now more or less knows how to do it. The real difficulty now is that AI makes large-scale statistical analysis, large-scale manipulation, large-scale opinion steering, and so on extremely easy, to the point where it seems to reach the level of international relations right from the outset.

So you don’t even know who originally set the whole thing in motion, and it has already passed through four, five, or six layers of agents. At each layer, they’ve effectively forgotten who gave the instruction upstream. Right now, we... I wrote an article called “Malicious AI Swarms,” which is about exactly this. When I wrote it, I didn’t yet know about the OpenAI incident, but clearly it was describing that very thing.

So if we want to solve this by applying the Civic AI framework we developed at Oxford, only two things really matter. One is to follow what’s called the principle of subsidiarity as much as possible: anything related to local, small-scale matters should be handled entirely by AI using local computing resources and local post-trained models. This is “as local as possible,” the sixth pack. The other is that everyone has the right to verify—and if they’re dissatisfied with that verification, the right to exit and switch to another system. With just these two, all the other rights can grow from them. An economist named [Elinor Ostrom](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/facts/) demonstrated that many commons can use a set of design principles to nest local self-governance into progressively larger scales, without needing a god in the heavens to resolve every dispute from the top down. You can have a whole lot of local gods cooperating with one another, but none of them looks after a space encompassing more than perhaps 150 people. I think this is the approach AI governance will inevitably move toward in the future.

Because the leaderboard dominance problem created by RLVR could produce superviruses, whether computer viruses, biological viruses, and so on. This problem—this problem is far too irreversible, so probably very few people would allow it to develop to that point. Things will almost certainly move in this direction: toward local, horizontal governance.
