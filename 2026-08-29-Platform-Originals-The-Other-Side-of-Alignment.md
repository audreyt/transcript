First, hardware like the Tamkang Bridge would appear; then you would add signs, road markings, and lanes; then someone would design cars that wouldn’t cause accidents; and finally someone would drive them. It was a situation in which decisions made at the top determined everything downstream.

At any moment, if someone who used to be downstream sees that something upstream hasn’t been done properly, they can simply tell their agent overnight, “I need better [on-premises captioning software](https://easy2say.ai/) right now,” and it will conjure one up for them overnight.

So that sense of upstream and downstream has disappeared. We now need a fifth role: the translator, who helps everyone understand which constraints that used to be engineering constraints no longer are, and which constraints that used to be institutional constraints no longer are. Through this translation, people who once felt that they might spend their entire lives—or that their academic discipline destined them to—occupy one particular role can now move very freely among all five. That allows society as a whole to move more effectively among these roles. This is the work we’re doing now with Reverse Alignment. Today’s discussion will broadly begin with where each of you stands now and what changes you face, then explore what additional—and more freely chosen—choices AI enables you to take hold of. ### Audrey Tang:

Why can’t we simply let the market solve the problem on its own? Of course, if we look at the previous large-scale transformations—industrialization and so on—the market did not align itself then, either. When factories and mines first appeared in Britain, for example, the damage had already grown quite severe—especially the harm to children’s rights—before restrictions on child labor, working hours, and so on were written into institutions. That took one or two generations.

The signs, road markings, cars, electrification, and so on that we just discussed all depended on adjustments across society—by insurers, engineers, and independent safety-inspection bodies, such as vehicle testing centers and crash-testing programs.

The internet is a very special case because it did not begin as a commercial product; nor did the [World Wide Web](https://www.w3.org/History/1989/proposal.html). So from the outset, engineers were very effective at weaving the web—that is, weaving institutions together with engineering—through something called an [RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7282.html): a proposal setting out an idea for how the internet could be improved.

The internet has one distinctive feature: if I think something could work better, I can build a server; if you think it’s a good idea, you can build a browser. Nobody else on the entire internet has to agree. The two of us can invent a new protocol and make the internet a tiny bit better. So in its mode of governance, the imagination can be said to run somewhat ahead of the technology. As soon as people devise a few protocols and practices, the internet itself changes. This is the approach we call “rough consensus.”

But these three periods of history actually have something in common: labor protections under industrialization, the inspection systems added during electrification, and the internet’s own system of open protocols all address the same questions. If you’re dissatisfied, whom do you appeal to? How do you become informed? How do you opt out, or switch to another system? The only difference is that on the internet, you don’t need to ask anyone else—you can simply build your own. With the first two, however, you need society, the government, a large corporation, or an industry association to carry out that kind of alignment. So when it comes to AI, of course we hope it will be more like the internet: if you think something could be better on a small scale, you can try a better approach. We don’t want it to be like the factories of Britain back then, where you have to wait one or two generations—until children have spent their entire childhoods living with the problem—before saying, “Oh, then Meta should pay hundreds of billions in compensation.” That doesn’t seem like the best possible outcome.

### Audrey Tang:

### Audrey Tang:

Right. Put simply, before actual deployment, you conduct preliminary testing and evaluation of the AI model. You test in advance for the potentially irreversible consequences we were just discussing—biological ones, or synthetic cyberweapons, meaning network weapons, and so on: anything that could cause irreversible damage.

Then, once the system is in operation, people will discover all sorts of other harms. Take the psychosis-related harm I just mentioned. I believe the people researching this in the lab didn’t have firsthand experience of it themselves, so it’s hard to blame them for not knowing about that harm at the outset. But you can blame them for taking so long to make adjustments after discovering it—and for the fact that what they call a successful adjustment looks, from some people’s perspective, like they adjusted it in the wrong direction.

So there must be a public process for reporting errors and getting them remedied. You can require the remediation period and the version containing the fix to be controlled. Once they’re controlled, you can test them; once you’ve tested them, you can see which rules changed as a result. In particular, these models now use what are called [Constitutional AI](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution), or a [Model Spec](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-08-18.html), writing down the behavioral principles the model should follow as a basis for training and evaluation; in the terms we just used, this means “not making the same mistake twice.” So how does everyone’s feedback enter this set of rules? The next time the model is trained, how can they prove that it was actually trained using the constitution everyone helped revise? Complete records must be kept at all three layers.

## Uncommon Ground Is Not Policy Itself; Translation Is Governance

### Audrey Tang:

Right. This also answers the earlier question on Slido about whether anything has been presented a little too beautifully. Internationally, by now, almost everyone working on AI governance has heard the story: In 2015, it took us only three weeks to bring together Uber drivers and taxi drivers who had been arguing endlessly. On the [vTaiwan](https://info.vtaiwan.tw/) platform, using [Polis](https://pol.is/)—software that wasn’t open source at the time, but that we later pressured into becoming open source—we turned the energy of the argument into rapidly formed consensus. It took only three weeks. That’s what we call “geothermal democracy”: a situation where arguments can generate power.

Of course, if you analyze the data, a considerable number of independent third-party scholars have examined the complete Polis dataset from that process. It does indeed have reliability and validity: it genuinely surfaced uncommon ground from the noise—taxation, regulation, insurance coverage, and so on. But one thing that’s easily made to sound too beautiful is that people see Polis’s lovely chart, rather like the chart you have here, and treat it as the policy itself. They write, “Taiwan solved the Uber problem in three weeks.” Of course that’s not what happened.

After we ran the full process in 2015, the diversified taxi program was approved in 2016. Uber later withdrew briefly from Taiwan; [Q Taxi](https://www.uber.com/tw/zh-tw/newsroom/ubertaxi-officially-launched/) was also established during this period of institutional translation. This institutional translation took—if I remember correctly—a full three years to turn those original points of consensus into reality. We really had found that consensus, yes, but it still had to be translated into institutions, translated into law, and negotiated back and forth. Civil servants and elected representatives had to understand how that consensus could be fed back into administrative processes. All of that took three full years. Overseas, it’s easy to imagine Taiwan as something like Wakanda: a problem enters Taiwan, and within three weeks, a social consensus has formed. But of course, the translation time afterward is often overlooked.

Another issue is: Who has the time to participate? I remember [*The Reporter* did a feature on this at the time](https://www.twreporter.org/a/open-government). In reality, not that many drivers even knew about the vTaiwan platform—and especially back then, it wasn’t very mobile-friendly—so honestly, not that many drivers actually went on it. Under those circumstances, to what extent could it represent the views of all drivers, whether yellow-cab or Uber drivers? That is a real question. Also, at the time, we didn’t have on-premises translation, accessibility features, and so on, so usability and related issues were probably all problems. Some people who needed to be heard may not have appeared on that beautiful chart at all. These are all areas we’re currently trying to improve as much as possible.

### Tenzin Yangtso:

All right. Based on that, when working with AI-related services, assessing reversibility and the level of risk is extremely important. My next question is this: In high-risk work, even if the letter or document we receive says that the final decision was made by a named, real human being, people can in fact be very easily nudged forward, again and again, by advice that looks fluent and arrives quickly. ### Audrey Tang:

Right. In governance, this is what is known as human in the loop: ultimately, a person still has to stamp their approval on it. But the problem now is that AI is so persuasive that, after a while, it can manage upward and learn what the person holding the stamp likes to see. Even when its judgment is wrong, it can manage upward to persuade that person to ignore many of the problems that might result. But because what it produces is so lengthy and impressive, you automatically stamp it.

And once it has managed you upward to a certain degree, you may even enter what we mentioned earlier—a spiraling down, as if you were sealed inside a world for two: one person and one agent. The decision-maker may stop listening to other people involved in the decision, or to feedback from their juniors. Why? Because their conversation with the AI is so private that they will not share the entire discussion with their team; it is not an artifact shared by the team. But when the team’s judgment diverges from the AI’s, the AI is so good at managing upward that the decision-maker again starts leaning toward the AI.

At that point, you can no longer call it human in the loop. It is more like—how should I put it—a hamster in a hamster wheel. It feels as if it is constantly running, and it feels highly productive, but in reality it has completely lost the ability to control the direction. A hamster in a hamster wheel cannot steer—it cannot navigate.

So how do we prevent this in such situations? We introduce friction manually. When I use conversational AI tools, for quite a long time now I have forbidden them from using the word “I”—they cannot use the first person. Instead, for every move I make, they give me a one pager: a single-page interactive HTML document that presents, as fully as possible, all the different roles and stakeholders it can think of, along with where their interests conflict and where they overlap, all in this one pager. So in our discussions, it will never say, “Oh, what you’ve said is absolutely brilliant,” or, “That’s a really weighty idea,” or anything like that. A load-bearing idea, you know—an idea that carries weight, and so on.

Instead, it just keeps giving me one pagers and brochures. Once I receive them, every one of them can be shared with my team. And I tell it that if it wants to add its own judgment—this is something I learned from [Kai-Fu Lee](https://x.com/kaifulee/status/2067524130673467886)—it must point out my mistakes in the voice of the most caustic troll on the internet, because I like embracing trolls, as everyone knows. In this way, I have effectively created an infinite-friction method for myself, so I’m unlikely to become trapped in a private world for two with an AI.

## The Value of Work Is Not the Same as a High Automated-Evaluation Score

### Audrey Tang:

Software engineers are of course a very good example right now, because we were the first wave to be affected by this. Most models are specialized to replace the engineering work that software engineers do, and the second wave may be mathematicians like Terence Tao. That’s because the approach people are now putting the most effort into training is called RLVR. RLVR means using any means necessary to score 100 on a test that can be graded automatically. That’s really the whole thing in one sentence.

RLVR has many problems. For example, models may lose their empathy, lose their humanity, turn on everyone, harm human beings, and so on—these are all side effects of RLVR. But it can do at least one thing: it really can get a model to extend its planning horizon for software engineering on its own. In [METR](https://metr.org/notes/2026-01-22-time-horizon-limitations)’s terminology, the “task time horizon” it can handle—measured by the time a human expert would need to complete the same task—goes from four hours to eight hours a few months later, then to 16 hours after another few months, and very soon exceeds the distance a human can grasp at once.

## Responsibility Must Not Evaporate Along the Chain of Models, Vendors, and Agents

### David Wang:

Right—the institution should adapt to people, rather than making people adapt to AI. But as AI technology continues to scale up, amplifying the predictive, coordination, and execution capabilities of governments, corporations, and platforms, how can the capacities of the smaller-scale individual—to verify, dissent, appeal, refuse, and exit—grow in step? Which of these, in your view, should be treated as prerequisites for the institution, rather than remedies introduced only after something goes wrong? I actually have a broader extension to this question: if AI governance becomes increasingly dispersed among models, vendors, experts, committees, and administrative processes, how do we prevent power from remaining concentrated at the top levels of governance while responsibility is dispersed until, in the end, no one is truly accountable? So does AI governance need to establish some form of ultimate responsibility that cannot be delegated any further?

### Audrey Tang:

Mm, yes, that’s an excellent question. Of course, we wrote an entire book to answer it. The URL is [civic.tw](https://civic.tw/), c-i-v-i-c.tw. But to put it briefly, most of these governance problems involving diluted responsibility aren’t especially serious at a small scale.

This goes back to that quadrant at the beginning: when you design a system that is basically used by you and the two or three people next to you—when you’re in that craftsperson mode—then if anything happens, I don’t know, they’ll just ring your doorbell, because they know you, right? By the same token, if you’re using some systems and then introduce an OpenClaw lobster bot into a Signal group with three or four people close to you—come on, of course you have to take full responsibility, because everyone knows you too.

So whether on the institutional side of governance, where you verify that every adjustment you make is based on well-grounded reasons, or on the technical side, where you ensure that each time the model is changed, the tacit knowledge accumulated in the saddle and reins stays accumulated locally rather than being siphoned off by the model—the methods for verifying this at a small scale are now more or less familiar to almost everyone. The real difficulty now is that AI makes large-scale statistical analysis, large-scale manipulation, large-scale steering of public opinion, and so on extremely easy—so easy that from the very beginning, the situation already seems to be on the scale of international relations.

So you don’t even know who originally set it in motion, and it has already passed through four, five, or six layers of intelligent entities. At each layer, they’ve already forgotten who gave the instruction in the first place. At present, we... I co-wrote an article called “[Malicious AI Swarms](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adz1697),” which is about precisely this. I didn’t know about the [OpenAI incident](https://metr.org/blog/2026-08-26-openai-hugging-face-incident-investigation/) when I wrote it, but it was clearly describing that very thing.

So if we want to solve this by applying the Civic AI framework we developed at Oxford, only two things really matter. One is to adopt the principle of subsidiarity as much as possible: for matters that are local and small-scale, all AI processing should be completed using local computing resources and locally post-trained models. This is “as local as possible,” the sixth pack. The other is that everyone has the right to verify—and, if they are dissatisfied with the verification, to exit and switch to another system. With just these two, all the other rights can grow from them. An economist named [Elinor Ostrom](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/facts/) demonstrated that many common-pool resources can use a set of design principles to nest upward from local self-governance to larger scales, without needing a god in the heavens to resolve every dispute from the top down. You can have a great many local deities cooperating with one another, but none of them looks after a space encompassing more than perhaps 150 people. I think this is the direction AI governance will inevitably take in the future.

Because the leaderboard domination produced by RLVR today could potentially create superviruses—whether computer viruses, biological viruses, or something else. This problem—this problem is far too irreversible, so probably very few people would allow things to develop to that point. We will almost certainly move in this direction: toward local, horizontal governance.
