## Opening: Civic AI and the 6-Pack of Care
### Audrey Tang:
Hello, many good friends and new friends. So, I have been developing here in Oxford for the past year or so, with a new book to be published by [Wiley](https://www.wiley.com/) next February, called the [6-Pack of Care](https://6pack.care/), where the 6-Pack represents portable but also muscular, as in abs, care. We call the AI systems that are aligned to this kind of care-ethics approach Civic AI. And a lot of the book, actually the entire book, is available in public domain on [civic.ai](https://civic.ai). So, I will not repeat it at length. I will just outline the main arguments and then we can go into discussion.

The observation is that when we discuss AI's trajectory in society, the default trajectory with current-day AI, with RL and so on, is what many people are now calling [solipsistic alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03237). That is to say, AI is single-mindedly pursuing some goals and seeing the world around them, including us humans, as environmental hazards. And in this world, even when AI is perfectly aligned to that particular goal, it still supercharges conflict. So, that is the default trajectory.

In Civic AI, we want an alternate trajectory where it helps our existing communities cooperate across differences. One side we can call singularity. The other side we call [Plurality](https://plurality.net/). So, this is the first claim.

And the second one is that currently we do not have a very good theory on how to keep a powerful general optimizer aligned with humanity. So, maybe do not even try. Maybe just have satisficers. Maybe do not do optimizers at all. So, when a system acts in a particular place, owing to existing relationships, and with humans forming the loop that we put the AI agents in — AI in the human loop, not human in the AI loop — that is a satisficing bounded agent. A little bit like [Drexler's comprehensive AI services](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:9c05427a-6390-4b42-9c55-ee45f73a26ad). A non-optimizing agent that satisfies this relationship. This is the second claim.

And so we claim that this kind of system gets its reward not from solipsistic speed, because in that speed, deontological commandments or consequentialist utility calculations become incommensurable with our current civilization. So, do not do that. Care ethics starts from a different picture where people remain each other's gardeners and AI becomes local infrastructure or scaffolding. So, this is what we call Kami, or knowledge artefact management intelligence — KM for knowledge management, AI for artificial intelligence. Put those two together and you get a Kami.

In Japanese folklore, there are 8 million [Kami](https://www.britannica.com/topic/kami). If you have watched [Spirited Away](https://www.ghibli.jp/works/chihiro/), you know that a village has a Kami, an [ujigami](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ujigami). A forest has a Kami. The sun has a Kami, [Amaterasu](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Amaterasu). But in Japanese folklore, there is no singleton. The Kami do not report to some higher-up. They are entirely federated, and each Kami is bounded. So, the health of the Kami of the forest is the health of that forest. Nothing more, nothing less. So, it is not power-seeking in the classical [reward-hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reward_hacking) kind of way.

So, consider a very concrete case, and then I will pause: Public service using AI.

In Taiwan, two years ago, we noticed that Facebook and YouTube had a lot more synthetic fraud advertisements than normal advertisements. It turns out their optimizer, their recommendation engine for advertisements, theorized that scammers pay much more pay-per-click than normal business. So, they featured a lot more scam than normal business. This is a classic case of a misaligned optimizer. And instead of putting censorship, because Taiwan is the most free in Asia when it comes to internet freedom, we simply randomly texted 200,000 people, our citizens, asking what is proportionate.

People gave us ideas, thousands of ideas, and many people volunteered. And then we chose randomly 447 people in a long afternoon. Each person over Zoom talking to 9 other people. We worked with [Stanford](https://moda.gov.tw/en/major-policies/alignment-assemblies/2024-deliberative-assembly/1521). The Kami, the Civic AI in this case, does not inject the judgment, but they do carry what is called the MIMBY, maybe-in-my-backyard, the middle-ground energy — not the [NIMBY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIMBY), not the outrage, the polarized energy — from one table to the other, cross-pollinating each other. Because the rule is that you have to get something the 9 other people can live with in order for your idea to bubble up.

So, for example, people say, let us put joint liability on Facebook: if they post an unsigned advertisement and somebody lost $7 million, then Facebook should pay $7 million. And another table says to their Kami that all advertisements should be digitally signed. If it is not signed, display like a cigarette label: probably scam. And another said [RedNote](https://www.xiaohongshu.com/), Xiaohongshu, at the time did not have a Taiwan office and ignored our liability laws. What to do? Every day they ignore us, slow down their video by 1 percent. Anyway, all these were considered proportionate by the particular Civic AI instances in those 44 tables, and instantly their minds merged together at the end of that long afternoon and people voted on it. These measures had more than 85 percent support. The other 15 percent can live with it, and it immediately became part of our draft law, and then it became law in two months.

So, throughout last year, 2025, there are almost no scam ads any more in Taiwan. Now it is down to 1 percent. Basically, it is a solved problem. So, it shows — that is a demo — that when people are given this steering wheel together to train an AI agent, then we do not need to involve people in continuous democracy 24/7. Nobody has time for that. But their preferences in the communal form, the relational form, can be imbued into those Kami agents who can then advocate for them and enter the lawmaking process and make the laws that draw not just the red lines, reining in misaligned recommenders, but also bright lines like the interoperable lines or evaluations. Of course, this is just one example.

## How the Alignment Assembly worked
### Another participant:
What you are describing sounds like narrow AIs rather than general ones. What I am unclear about is how exactly they are built. Are they still LLMs, just with a very narrow training set?

### Audrey Tang:
That is exactly right. There is a set of narrow models. Some of them start with a language model such as [Gemma](https://ai.google.dev/gemma), or now something called [Snowdon](https://huggingface.co/tri-fair-lab/Snowdon1.1-Small), which is tuned by Imperial College London. But then we narrow it to particular purposes such as summarization, preference aggregation, and translation, so that we only specialize that particular narrow part. And because of that, it can run on edge hardware very easily. I run multiple of those on my MacBook currently using Ollama, and then the conductor model can look at your incoming request and route to one of those narrow models.

### Participant:
So underneath, it is still a language model — and therefore generalizable — but it is made narrow by training on a narrow dataset.

### Audrey Tang:
And also we expose only a very narrow tool surface to it.

### Participant:
I see. So it is still a general AI, but trained and used in a narrow way.

### Audrey Tang:
Well, it starts with a general generative-AI base, but it is so specialized that it is not very clear whether it can form its own goals. Because all the long-term planning, whatever, that is still communal. So, it is a glorified chess clock, essentially.

### Another participant:
In the Taiwan example you described, may I understand the exact process? People were contacted, and so on — what happened in more detail?

### Audrey Tang:
The full process, called the Alignment Assemblies, starts with an open-ended poll. Anyone who has an idea of how AI can enter society positively in 2040 can just write a short sentence as a kind of vision statement, so we know where they are coming from. And then from their vision statements, they can look at other people's statements and upvote or downvote them. There is no reply button, so there is no room for trolls to grow. What we get is essentially k-means clusters. You can see people with various different predilections on how AI and society interact, and also what we call the bridging bonus. The more people come up with statements that convince the other side, the more bridging bonus is assigned. So, we can get the most uncommon ground across all the different ideologies. And then from there, we look at the issues people consider as urgent. For example, deepfake fraud, that is one. And then we do a random [sortition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortition) to send 200,000 random messages.

### Participant:
Sorry — just to get the mechanics: how many people were involved at the start? And did each of them upvote or downvote the others?

### Audrey Tang:
In the very beginning, this is self-selecting. It is open participation, anybody can join. We got easily thousands. Each person can submit multiple statements, and the aggregation is not by voting, it is by the upvotes and downvotes — each person's reaction to each other — as much as they want. For the Uber case in 2015, it was a three-week process before we could get a set of, I think it was 7 statements that consistently got more than 85 percent from both clusters, from the taxi and the Uber drivers.

### Participant:
And once that was done, could you fill in the remaining steps? You said it ultimately led to legislation — could you take us through it slowly?

### Audrey Tang:
Yes, definitely. The initial open-ended poll generated the ideology clusters as well as their visions. And one of the unifying bridging statements across different ideologies was that the short-term fraud and synthetic intimacy and other information harms need to be tackled by the government before we can even consider any medium-term or long-term risk. If we lose the ability to trust each other online, we cannot coordinate on other risks. That is the number-one unifying argument from the first phase.

And so then we decided to commit ourselves. I was minister then. We said, okay, then we will ask a representative selection, a stratified random sample — a rigorous sample of Taiwan’s people. First we sampled 200,000 random numbers around Taiwan, so people receive in their SMS an invitation. They can choose to ignore us, of course, but they at least know it comes from me because it is [111](https://moda.gov.tw/press/press-releases/16458). The sending number is the government's number; it is not a scammer. And if they decide to contribute, they fill in their profile — age bracket, place they live, education level, gender, whatever — and we ensure the final selection of 447 is a statistical mirror of Taiwan’s population, so that we can show our legislature that this is the will of our people.

Once they propose their ideas and we sync up schedules, we pay for their time to participate in that long day of deliberation. It is a little bit like jury duty. Each person looks at 9 other people in a room of 10. There are 44 such rooms, and each person needs to get their idea across so the other 9 people at least can live with it. This encourages not the NIMBY, but the MIMBY — maybe in my backyard, if I do this, if you do that, and so on. After each table bubbles up, a real-time Kami Civic AI takes transcripts and encourages quiet people to speak up and limits disruption to five seconds and so on. That is the chess-clock part, and then it aggregates.

### Participant:
So you have juries of 10 people meeting with AI agents that act, essentially, as moderators or facilitators.

### Audrey Tang:
That is exactly right. It is not inserting any agenda. It is just keeping a Habermasian situation for those 10 people. And there are 44 rooms concurrently.

### Participant:
And what was the next layer?

### Audrey Tang:
During the plenary, if the Civic AI determined that there are unclear factual questions to put to the legislator — sorry, the regulator, the ministry people, or the experts — then they answer it, and then they go back to the same room and enter the next round of conversation to come up with a concrete proposal on what is proportionate.

Very interestingly, what started as a very popular or even populist proposal — for example, forcing Facebook to open-source the entire algorithm, the model weights of their ad recommendation engine — that started very popular, I think 80 percent for. But then after deliberation and after the expert response, people saw that if you open it, then scammers will adversarially train against it. It is actually not a good idea. If you only open it to, say, five experts, you create moral hazard for those five experts. So, it actually fell more than 25 points during this conversation. So, you can rein in some populist ideas too. At the end, people voted on all the ideas that survived those different tables. Each table voted on other tables' surviving ideas. By the end, we have a concrete slate of policy that we can show 85 percent like and 15 percent can live with.

### Participant:
Sorry — was there a plenary with free discussion among all 440-odd people, or did only the ideas flow between rooms?

### Audrey Tang:
The rooms are organisms. There are no individuals as such. Even in the published report, we say that this room came up with this consensus. The dynamic in the room is first-class, not the individuals. A room can vote on, for example, the crux that they must pose to the experts, in which case the experts respond to all 44 rooms during the intermediate plenary.

### Participant:
So the idea is to take a human group small enough for coherent dialogue, and keep deliberation at that level.

### Audrey Tang:
That is exactly right. And then after that, I come up and put my name to their suggestions, explaining for each one why we adopt this in the law. Or, why this cannot yet be adopted — actually, I did not do any of that — I adopted everything into the law. And because at the time, when the parliament received our draft law, there was no majority party — all three parties had less than 50 percent — but because we already showed this is what people want, they fast-tracked that particular law and it passed in just two months.

### Participant:
Very nice. Returning to the larger vision: how do you see this scaling up to more powerful jurisdictions — perhaps even to more powerful countries?

## Scaling out
### Audrey Tang:
Concurrently to the 2023 and 2024 Alignment Assemblies, we ran with [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/democratic-inputs-to-ai/) and Anthropic the same process. One thousand randomly selected U.S. citizens [co-created part of the constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/news/collective-constitutional-ai-aligning-a-language-model-with-public-input) that went into Anthropic Claude 3, including people with handicaps, universal access and things like that that were omitted in the Claude 2 constitution document. So, that went okay, and [Wojciech Zaremba](https://woj.world/) of OpenAI also funded 10 different teams, including the [vTaiwan](https://vtaiwan.tw/) team, to run similar processes for what they now call the model spec. At least both labs see the model-spec layer as something very much amenable to this process.

And now we are seeing Japan, for example. There is a national party, the Future Party, [Team Mirai](https://team-mir.ai/), that was formed literally from the book we co-wrote, Plurality, as their platform — to use this method to crowdsource their platform. They do not have a party platform as such. They only have a technical platform called [Idobata](https://dd2030.org/idobata/), where people can just talk to their AI agent and debate it. If the person gets their arguments in, then they update their platform based on people's ideas. The party was very successful, and the head of the party, [Takahiro Anno](https://team-mir.ai/member/sangiin/takahiro-anno), is now getting a lot of young people's support, but is also a kind of tutor to their Prime Minister, [Takaichi](https://www.sanae.gr.jp/)-san. So, the LDP, the ruling party, does not need to go the populist way, but can be more popular than populism by directly talking to the young people themselves. That is working very well in Japan.

And in California, we just got the [third reading](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billHistoryClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB2103) — the institutionalization of this process, called [Engaged California](https://engaged.ca.gov/), to the California legislature. It is on Governor Newsom's desk now, so hopefully he will sign it soon. He is very likely to sign it soon. The same process in California has been used to talk about wildfire prevention and mitigation after the [Eaton](https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents/2025/1/7/eaton-fire) and [Palisade wildfire](https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents/2025/1/7/palisades-fire), about government employees' use of AI, about government efficiency like [DOGE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Government_Efficiency) — with a chain reaction, not the chainsaw, and now with all California citizens on the impact of AI on work.

The idea is that California wants to use this methodology to create what is called a renewed [Social License to Operate](https://socialicense.com/definition.html), or SLO, for frontier AI labs. Not only the existing — Governor Newsom just signed, as you know, the inspection and independent verification of frontier labs. But maybe you can imagine that the Californian people would want, through this process, to force the frontier labs to spend their very sizable compute not just to solve the [Millennium Prize](https://www.claymath.org/millennium-problems/), although I like it, but to solve issues really concerning Californian residents, so that they can earn a kind of positive dividend every week — and again draw not just the red line, but also the bright line of AI: the guide rail, not just the guardrail.

## Distributed intelligence vs singleton
### Another participant:
Underlying this whole vision, I would say, is an assumption that we are heading towards a world of distributed artificial intelligences: many different ones, fairly narrow in scope, each of which can be aligned to a small group of people. That is a very different technical premise from the world of a so-called singleton, where one AI develops overwhelming capability. I do not have strong views on which world we are heading towards; it turns on how large the returns to recursive self-improvement really are, and on how much difference intelligence makes. If a runaway capability gap opens between the leading frontier AI and all the others, then all of this becomes irrelevant. But perhaps that is not where we end up. Perhaps we get something closer to Iain Banks’s Culture novels — many, many AI agents, different but broadly comparable, with overlapping capabilities — and then this discussion of how to align them is the one we need.

### Audrey Tang:
Yes, definitely. The singleton world is winner-takes-all. We are essentially betting on one alignment team winning, but the leading alignment teams are like, actually, we are not sure any of us is winning. So, I think a reframe — especially this week with Coxon's actions — we are really looking at a reframe from a winner-take-all race. Like, okay, then the robot that destroyed cyberspace speaks English, not Mandarin. Okay, is that a trophy? Maybe not a trophy. So, then we need a really good reframe that is not just saying this is a suicide race, but rather there is now a mission. Whereas a race asks only who is winning, a mission can ask who is still missing, who is left behind.

The exact same [RSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive_self-improvement) techniques can be used for what I call recursive selfless improvement. Instead of making a single model even more solipsistic and goal-seeking, each turn can make the cooperation between existing communities even stronger, so that you can bridge over even longer ideological gaps. Some people call this super-cooperation at the [Cooperative AI Foundation](https://www.cooperativeai.com/foundation). The same [mechanistic interpretability](https://transformer-circuits.pub/), the same RSI — like shrinking the ability to fit into very small RAMs and not high-bandwidth RAMs and so on. All those technical advances can be done in a distributed way that fosters the configuration of plurality, not singularity.

In fact, we all benefit from the recent auto-research. I am part of the auto-research swarm called [Yukon](https://yukon.org/), where we donate our idle MacBook compute together into an open research agenda, so that we speed up the running of those models on Apple Silicon as compared to Nvidia, because Nvidia is very power-concentrating and Apple Silicon as currently produced is very distributed. If we can convince people that running inference through [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/) on that spare idle Apple Silicon compute is both faster and cheaper and higher quality than concentrated Nvidia chips, Nvidia itself will actually change direction toward this more distributed intelligence.

### Another participant:
I follow that idea. But how does it connect to the process you ran? How would people plug into this — how would the training process change to take account of the human collectivity you have in mind?

### Audrey Tang:
A good example is that I have been helping both [Bluesky](https://bsky.social/) and X, previously Twitter, to improve their recommendation systems. Remember, 10 years ago, that was the originally misaligned optimizer that created so many problems around the world. So, we are trying to help them fix that.

On the Bluesky side, there is a system now I use daily called [MySky](https://mysky.social/). What it does is it looks at the communities that I am part of, and works on the bridging, so that the post that unites the various communities I am part of, where they usually fight — the crux — they identify the kind of post that will elicit upvotes from both sides. A kind of healing post. It is also healing for me, because if I see it all the time, I do not get pulled by all the different tensions of my identities. And you can dial how much bridging and how much balancing you want to assign. The paper describing this is called [Community by Design](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10834). It is a preprint, but anyway, we designed that. So, now Bluesky will probably endorse that.

At the same time, I work with Keith Coleman and Jay Baxter, working with Elon's team to open-source their training pipeline for the [X recommendation engine](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm), because they are also developing a system called [Community Notes](https://communitynotes.x.com/guide/en/about/introduction). It is an open-source system where if Elon or anyone posts something that is wrong, immediately you can see [Grok](https://x.ai/grok), the language model, drafting something that will restore the balance for the post with other communities. And then people on X upvote or downvote various different candidates — not necessarily drafted by Grok, maybe by other Kami systems. The candidate that gets the most upvotes from opposing ideologies floats to the top and becomes sticky to the post.

So, now they are working to change their training pipeline so that you can see the common knowledge first. Elon would wake up and look at the first post as the most bridging, with an attached message saying that this is agreed by everyone in the U.S., for example, or by different sides, and so on. The idea is by changing his information diet, we can start a redemption cycle for him, but also make it much easier for people to serve as a bridge. Eventually, Truth Social or Bluesky or various other communities do not have to federate all their posts, but they can share and tap into the same contextualization system. This is called reinforcement learning by community feedback, [RLCF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.24118). And it is not so solipsistic, because every turn you actually get the firehose on how people are reacting to you.

One clear thing is that this needs to be an incompletely theorized action. This is not us making a compromise. A good example: in Taiwan we have e-petition, so 5,000 e-petitions can force a ministerial response. One day we had 8,000 people clamouring that we change our time zone to the same as Japan, +9, because they want to force people travelling from Beijing to change their clock to show sovereignty or uniqueness or whatever. And on the other side, 8,000 people also petitioned to keep our time zone at +8. So, now we have 16,000 people all very angry at each other. The problem here cannot be solved by making a compromise. If we change the time zone to 8.5 hours, it will just offend everybody. Instead, we look at the underlying pattern — because we do not have a reply button, we do not have a retweet button — we can only propose more and more nuanced statements for finer and finer resolution for people to agree on both sides.

We eventually converged, kind of hill-climbed, on the idea that we should spend some money to make Taiwan seen as more unique in the world compared to Beijing. But we also calculated exactly how much one hour of time change will cost the society. And we brainstormed with people on both sides in person on how to make more effective use of our budget to make Taiwan seem unique in the world. And then we decided to give [Gold Cards](https://goldcard.nat.gov.tw/en/) — global talent to open-source makers — to hold a human-rights conference, [RightsCon](https://www.rightscon.org/), to amplify our marriage equality, whatever — at a fraction of the cost but much more durable than a time-zone change.

The point here is that if we condense to a referendum — if Swiss democracy is no canton assembly, just referendum — then of course the dynamic you mentioned will happen: the extreme side will force, like a balance report of a populist party, like [AfD](https://www.afd.de/) or whatever, will dominate this balancing algorithm. But because this is an open poll with a continuous resolution, not a once-every-four-year or two-year resolution, then over time, a very quick hill-climb on something that the original group does not have to yield — it ends up with an action that can be incompletely theorized and supported by the extreme side as well. So, it leaves everybody happier. But it only happens if you allow for a Kami-like high-bandwidth social translation in the connective tissue, instead of a scalar mapping to referenda.
