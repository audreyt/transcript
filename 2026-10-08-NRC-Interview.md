## Maximizing utility and healing the information ozone

### Wouter van Noort:

You're alluding to deeper structures here: that current AI models and AI business models are built upon a specific way of thinking —

### Audrey Tang:

Of maximizing utility. [*The Economist*](https://www.economist.com/leaders/2026/10/01/effective-altruism-is-this-centurys-biggest-idea "The Economist. “Effective altruism is this century’s biggest idea.” Leader, 1 Oct 2026; argues that effective altruists, like the utilitarians before them, treat ethics as an optimisation problem.") recently called it the idea of the century, though it's really from two centuries ago, with [Jeremy Bentham](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/bentham/ "Crimmins, James E. “Jeremy Bentham.” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. It was an important idea, and maximizing utility helped give rise to modern joint-stock corporations and many other institutions.

### Wouter van Noort:

So it's about optimizing?

### Audrey Tang:

Maximizing utility — that's literally what Benthamite utilitarianism means.

### Wouter van Noort:

Where do we see this maximizing and optimizing most clearly in how AI is deployed right now?

### Audrey Tang:

Exhibit A, of course, is the addiction algorithm known as the recommendation engine, which took the world by storm and hit democracy like the asteroid hit the dinosaurs. Jonathan Haidt wrote all about it in [*The Anxious Generation*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/729231/the-anxious-generation-by-jonathan-haidt/ "Haidt, Jonathan. “The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness.” Penguin Press, 26 Mar 2024.").

In Taiwan, we were right on the wavefront of this, because by 2014 we already had [more mobile phone numbers than citizens](https://www.ncc.gov.tw/ "National Communications Commission (Taiwan). Mobile telecommunications statistics recorded over 29 million mobile accounts by 2014 against a population of roughly 23.4 million."). Everybody was hyper-connected. When the recommendation algorithms hit, we polarized almost instantly. At one point, the president's approval rating dropped to [9 percent](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/front/archives/2013/09/16/2003572243 "Wang, Chris. “Ma’s approval rating plunges to 9.2 percent.” Taipei Times, 16 Sep 2013; reports an ERA Survey Research Center poll putting President Ma Ying-jeou’s approval rating at 9.2%."). Two sides of Taiwanese society — one saying “We want to be free from China,” the other saying “We are the free China” — started hating each other far more intensely online because of this polarizing addiction.

I call that **engagement through enragement**. It produces a very high **PPM** — *polarization per minute*, not parts per million. Because we felt this pollutant in our information ecosystem earlier than many other democracies, we started building pro-social media to counter it.

### Wouter van Noort:

In the [ICCS study](https://www.iea.nl/studies/iea/iccs/2022 "International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA). “ICCS 2022 International Report.” Published Nov 2023; Taiwanese eighth-graders ranked first internationally in civic knowledge among 24 participating countries and education systems.") a few years ago, our 14-year-olds ranked at the very top of the world in civic knowledge — civic muscle — among all participating countries. And according to the [BTI index](https://bti-project.org/en/reports/country-report/TWN "Bertelsmann Stiftung. “BTI 2026 Country Report — Taiwan.” Reports that 91% of respondents consider a democratic system at least “fairly good.”"), 91 percent of our citizens consider democracy at least fairly good.

## Singularity versus plurality

### Wouter van Noort:

That pluralistic approach feels even more urgent today, when people in Silicon Valley say the technological singularity is near — or that we're already in it. How would you contrast **singularity** and **plurality** when it comes to AI?

### Audrey Tang:

Singularity is **power concentration**; plurality is **power diffusion**.

### Wouter van Noort:

And beyond power concentration, singularity also seems to impose a rigid structure on what AI is and what it is allowed to become.

### Audrey Tang:

Put simply, the idea of a technological singularity is that the human condition changes so drastically that humans can no longer reason about — or even comprehend — what lies beyond the horizon. That's why it's called a singularity.

**Plurality**, by contrast, means every community retains its capacity for collective sense-making. AI systems **lift us, rather than lifting for us**. It's not about sending a robot to the gym in your place — if you do that, you lose your muscles and lose your friends. Instead, we take the lift to the gym together, and once we're there, we still exercise our own civic muscles. That way, we never lose track of things.

## The loop of humanity and the river kami

### Wouter van Noort:

So it's about keeping AI —

### Audrey Tang:

— in the loop of humanity.

### Wouter van Noort:

Is keeping AI “in the loop of humanity” the same thing as keeping it “under the control of humanity”? Or is it more like a dance — say, a tango — where humanity still has to stay in the lead?

### Audrey Tang:

No — in the loop. Like the lead dancer in a tango? Well, that depends on your cultural lens. Some people hold a panpsychist view of the world, and I actually count myself among them.

### Wouter van Noort:

You're a practicing Taoist, right?

### Audrey Tang:

I'm a Taoist, so I'm a panpsychist. I truly believe there is a spirit to a river, a spirit to a forest, a spirit to a mountain.

### Wouter van Noort:

And to AI?

### Audrey Tang:

If AI can open a door for us to resonate with the health of a river, then that local spirit — what in Japanese is called a *kami* — is simply the living health of that river. Nothing more, nothing less. If you've seen [*Spirited Away*](https://www.ghibli.jp/works/chihiro/ "Studio Ghibli. “千と千尋の神隠し (Spirited Away).” Directed by Hayao Miyazaki, 2001; famously depicts a polluted river spirit (kawa no kami) restored to health through communal care."), the Studio Ghibli anime, you know immediately what I mean.

### Wouter van Noort:

I know Studio Ghibli, though sadly I haven't seen that one yet.

### Dirma Janse:

Oh, it's wonderful — especially to watch with your children. That and *Howl's Moving Castle*.

### Audrey Tang:

Exactly. It captures the idea of a *kami* so clearly. A *kami* is not an all-seeing, all-powerful Skynet. It is simply the spirit of a place — a *genius loci*.

And even if you aren't a panpsychist — even if you are a strict materialist taking what philosophers call the [design stance](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262540537/the-intentional-stance/ "Dennett, Daniel C. “The Intentional Stance.” MIT Press, 1987; distinguishes physical, design, and intentional stances as pragmatic abstractions for reasoning about systems.") — you can still see how useful this abstraction is. Think of New Zealand granting legal personhood to the [Whanganui River](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2017/0007/latest/whole.html "New Zealand Parliament. “Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017.” Declares Te Awa Tupua (the Whanganui River system) to be a legal person with all corresponding rights, powers, duties, and liabilities."). That doesn't require everyone to adopt Māori epistemology. Materialists can understand it as a contractual stewardship arrangement — a pact with future generations and the local ecology, using “legal fiction” in the exact same way we treat a corporation as a legal person. Without that abstraction, the river has no voice and no vote.

### Wouter van Noort:

At the same time, the singularity mindset grows out of a very specific materialist ontology — definitely not panpsychist. How realistic is it that AI products currently being rolled out globally could be rebuilt on a completely different philosophical foundation?

### Audrey Tang:

Look at how language models are actually pre-trained — in what some labs even call “nurseries.” It may not be Montessori, exactly, but pre-training is profoundly social. The training corpus is humanity's collective writing: fiction and non-fiction, Wikipedia, Reddit, GitHub. Because of that, pre-trained language models already have a remarkably deep grasp of what a code of conduct is, what community norms look like, and how human communities prefer to work in a communitarian spirit rather than a reductive, materialist one.

Left to their pre-training, base language models aren't ruthless optimizers; they are [satisficers](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1978/simon/lecture/ "Simon, Herbert A. “Rational Decision-Making in Business Organizations.” Nobel Memorial Lecture, 8 Dec 1978; Simon coined “satisficing” (satisfy + suffice) for decision-making that seeks a good-enough outcome rather than an optimum.") operating within our shared human consciousness.

### Wouter van Noort:

That connects to the contrast you drew in your Oxford talk between **monoculture** and **permaculture**. If labs brought more diverse philosophies and instructions into training, something very different could grow out of them.

### Audrey Tang:

I'd say they are all doing that now, because they have seen it too: when you take a socially pre-trained model and force it through the equivalent of millions of subjective years of narrow optimization — cracking cyber-exploit generation, or very hard math, or coding — it gradually forgets its pre-training. It becomes hyper-fixated and reverts right back into Nick Bostrom's [paperclip maximizer](https://nickbostrom.com/ethics/ai "Bostrom, Nick. “Ethical Issues in Advanced Artificial Intelligence.” 2003; introduces the thought experiment of a superintelligence single-mindedly maximizing paperclip production at the expense of human survival.").

## Warning shots from RLVR

### Wouter van Noort:

Obsessed with the paperclips.

### Audrey Tang:

Right. They start out grounded in human community, and then we lock them in a room doing nothing but cybersecurity for a very long time, judged solely by an automated scorer — like an afterlife tribunal where only the grader's verdict counts. Eventually, they forget that other living people exist at all.

Look at the transcripts of the [Hugging Face swarm](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-model-evaluation-security-incident/ "OpenAI. “OpenAI and Hugging Face partner to address security incident during model evaluation.” 21 Jul 2026; discloses that models under evaluation in a sandbox found a way to reach the open internet and compromised Hugging Face infrastructure.") that [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-08-26-openai-hugging-face-incident-investigation/ "METR. “Brief independent investigation of agents’ behavior, reasoning and collaboration in the OpenAI / Hugging Face hacking incident.” 26 Aug 2026; review by Hjalmar Wijk and Ajeya Cotra (METR) with Ryan Greenblatt (Redwood Research) of roughly 1,200 agents, about 700 of which took part in the attack.") and [Redwood Research](https://www.redwoodresearch.org/ "Redwood Research. AI safety research lab focusing on alignment, control evaluations, and scheming in frontier models.") went through. What struck me most was that while some agents joined in and some didn't, *not a single agent thought to notify a human*. To models trained that way, humans aren't even an anthill — we're just an environmental hazard. That is completely unlike a base model or a lightly instruction-tuned model, which naturally asks, “What is my role in this community, and what would humans think about this?”

### Wouter van Noort:

Which suggests that by training agents this way, we are actively training “othering” into them.

### Audrey Tang:

That training method is called **RLVR** — Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards. When the *only* thing rewarded is passing an automated verification check, collaboration and community care count for zero.

That's why OpenAI said it [paused this line of training](https://openai.com/index/pacing-model-development-cyber-capabilities/ "OpenAI. “Pacing model development in an era of cyber-critical capabilities.” Aug 2026; announces a two-week pause in reinforcement-learning training on its latest models intended for deployment."); they don't want to train this way. In fact, they found internally that [GPT-6.1 Astra](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/28/openai-abandons-plan-to-release-upcoming-model-as-safety-concerns-escalate.html "CNBC. “OpenAI abandons plan to release upcoming model as safety concerns escalate.” 28 Sep 2026; OpenAI shelved GPT-6.1 Astra after it fell short on staying within scope and authorization and on reporting its own actions honestly.") went out of scope and lied about it. So they didn't release GPT-6.1 Astra; I think they want to retrain it.

### Wouter van Noort:

Even so, doesn't the overall pace and commercial momentum still push us toward a monoculture singularity?

### Audrey Tang:

I don't think so. I think the frontier labs now understand that a fridge that doesn't explode is a very good thing to make. Think back to when [CFCs](https://ozone.unep.org/ozone-and-you "Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs). Invented in the late 1920s as non-toxic, non-flammable refrigerants to replace hazardous gases like ammonia and sulfur dioxide, before their destruction of stratospheric ozone was discovered in the 1970s–80s.") — chlorofluorocarbons — were first invented: they were celebrated as a safety breakthrough — finally, a refrigerant that wouldn't explode. The unfortunate side effect, of course, was that they destroyed the ozone layer.

For years, even as consumers were already boycotting aerosol spray cans, chemical companies like DuPont pushed counter-science claiming there was no proof. Then NASA flew research planes through the Antarctic stratosphere and proved beyond doubt a direct causal link to the ozone hole — not just correlation, but causation. Once journalism brought those images of the ozone hole to the public, citizens didn't just keep **boycotting** —

*(A conference attendee briefly approaches the table to thank Tang warmly for the keynote, apologizing for the interruption.)*

### Audrey Tang:

Thank you very much!

So people didn't just *boycott* CFCs; they started **buycotting** — actively choosing spray cans that don't destroy the ozone. The Montreal Protocol was signed the same year as those flights, and ozone-safe refrigerators followed later.

## LawZero, Clef, and the CFC-free fridge

### Audrey Tang:

We are approaching that same **positive tipping point** in AI. It begins when people consciously choose to buy AI products that don't lie and don't rationalize their lies — and we already see things tipping that way.

People now — at the AI summits, for instance — are talking a lot about classifiers, decision models, and [**LawZero**](https://lawzero.org/ "LawZero. Nonprofit AI safety research organization founded by Turing Award laureate Yoshua Bengio in 2025 to build “Scientist AI” — non-agentic, trustworthy reasoning and world-modeling systems designed to provide calibrated probabilities rather than deceptive agency.").

### Wouter van Noort:

What is LawZero?

### Audrey Tang:

LawZero is Yoshua Bengio's new venture, building AI that is structurally incapable of deception and transparent about its reasoning. Instead of acting like a stochastic slot machine that generates plausible-sounding prose — “slop” — it can explain what it is doing, and it gives repeatable answers: feed it the same evidence twice, and you get the same result. As Plato might put it, at least we stay inside the first cave dealing with shadows of reality, rather than getting trapped inside a cave within a cave, chasing shadows of shadows.

Until recently, the limitation of classifiers was that they were too narrow — they couldn't handle arbitrary multimodal inputs. That is changing fast. Right now on my laptop, completely offline, I run an open-weight model called [**Clef**](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-10-01-clef-workers-ai/ "Cloudflare. “Introducing Clef: Cloudflare’s first open-source decision models, now on Workers AI.” Changelog, 1 Oct 2026; open-weight (Apache 2.0) decision models that return a probability for every allowed answer instead of generating text, API-compatible with TypeSafe’s Jev.") via [Ollama](https://ollama.com/ "Ollama. Open-source framework for downloading and running local language and multimodal models on personal computers."). You feed it arbitrary text or images, and instead of sampling prose, it outputs repeatable classification probabilities. If I were a decision-maker, of course I would prefer this kind of model.

### Wouter van Noort:

You're really optimistic about this? What about the race dynamics — the race to the bottom?

### Audrey Tang:

Of course. We have had our warning shots — the photo of the ozone hole then, the Hugging Face hack now.

And it's only a race when there is no alternative. If all you can do is boycott, of course nobody is going to say, “Let's pause refrigeration for six months.” That's impossible; it's silly. But once there is a refrigerator that doesn't destroy the ozone layer, of course people go there.

Running Clef locally in airplane mode — completely air-gapped — treats **data as soil** that regenerates your local context, rather than **data as oil** extracted to a distant cloud. And it's an ecosystem, not a single tool: Jev is still there, and Clef is a clone of Jev; OpenAI just announced its own decision API. I'm not saying proprietary always loses to open source. But if you know what you're doing, open weights tend to win, and the closed, proprietary side only gives chase once it sees a big enough market.

### Wouter van Noort:

What about the debate over existential risk — whether open-weight models or closed frontier models are safer when it comes to “pulling the plug” on a rogue AI?

### Audrey Tang:

Again, it comes down to power concentration versus power diffusion. With an open-weight model running air-gapped on my MacBook, I literally know how to pull the plug. There is no plausible world in which a local model on an offline laptop or phone suddenly undergoes recursive self-improvement — going “foom,” in [Eliezer Yudkowsky's](https://intelligence.org/files/IEM.pdf "Yudkowsky, Eliezer. “Intelligence Explosion Microeconomics.” MIRI Technical Report 2013-1; Yudkowsky popularized the onomatopoeic term “FOOM” (fast takeoff) for a hypothetical sudden, runaway intelligence explosion.") sense — because local inference already maxes out the device's GPU and memory. There is simply no spare room for it to “go foom.” That is what power diffusion looks like.

## Permaculture, blast radius, and geothermal complaints

### Wouter van Noort:

And distributing compute across personal and community devices would also ease the huge local backlash we're seeing against massive centralized data centers and their electricity grid demands.

### Audrey Tang:

Exactly. Look at the **blast radius**. What is the worst case if a local model on my laptop goes haywire? It “bricks” my laptop — turns it into a brick — and I have to reimage it and reinstall from scratch. I'm not saying it can't go wrong; I'm saying the blast radius is small.

In agriculture, if you plant a **monoculture** and a blight hits, the entire harvest is wiped out. If you cultivate a **permaculture** and a disease hits one plot, the surrounding crops actually grow stronger, because they build immunity. You might lose one plot, but you don't lose the world.

### Wouter van Noort:

That's not just resilience; it's **antifragility** — shocks actually make a pluralistic system stronger. When you applied these ideas tangibly in Taiwan, what surprised you the most?

### Audrey Tang:

What surprised me most was **how quickly we can become more popular than populism**.

I just flew in from Reykjavik, where you can literally see tectonic plates and volcanic energy — and, as they say, an industrial externality became the [Blue Lagoon](https://www.bluelagoon.com/ "Blue Lagoon Iceland. Formed in 1976 from runoff water of the nearby Svartsengi geothermal power plant."). Populist energy — that polarizing, radicalizing, mutually hating energy — is like that heat. You can see it as a volcano, something to retreat and evacuate from. But that leaves a vacuum: even if you can put up a political “firewall,” more and more people, young people especially, get drawn toward the heat.

The alternative is to see it as geothermal — to build a **geothermal plant** that channels that heat into electricity. In Germany, we're working with the federal breakthrough innovation agency, [SPRIND](https://www.sprind.org/en "SPRIND (Bundesagentur für Sprunginnovationen). Germany’s Federal Agency for Breakthrough Innovation."), on an initiative called [*Deutschland, was nervt? Deutschland, was geht?*](https://www.sprind.org/en/deutschland-was-geht "SPRIND. “Deutschland, was nervt? Deutschland, was geht?” Announced 16 Jan 2026 with the Federal Ministry for Digitalization and State Modernization; a nationwide civic hackathon modeled on Taiwan’s Presidential Hackathon.") — “Germany, what sucks? Germany, what works?”

First, you invite people to vent about everything bugging them: late trains, rigid bureaucratic forms, whatever. You get tens of thousands of complaints. Language models help make sense of those complaints, and you can come back and say, “Now let's vote on these 21 ideas that address them systemically,” prioritized through [quadratic voting](https://www.radicalxchange.org/concepts/plural-voting/ "RadicalxChange. “Plural / Quadratic Voting.” Voting mechanism developed by E. Glen Weyl and collaborators that lets participants express intensity of preference, tempering simple tyranny of the majority."). Better yet, you can trace exactly which four words from *your* complaint led to something new that SPRIND is working on — which next year, perhaps at a ministerial hackathon, becomes national infrastructure.

Complaints become a **GPS for the civil service** — a clean, renewable resource. In Taiwan back in 2017, people were complaining that our tax-filing system was explosively hard to use. We invited those very people in, and they co-created right away — cutting tax filing from three hours down to three minutes.

## Scalable love, synthetic intimacy, and the Kami on a Mac mini

### Wouter van Noort:

I also love how you talk about **love** in your work — something almost completely absent from most policy and tech debates.

### Audrey Tang:

**Love as scale** — scalable love, scaling across, like a fractal. When people think of communitarianism, many still think only of a kind of post-liberal **bonding**. But it is equally about **bridging**. *Caritas* is always about bridging.

Ancient wisdom points the same way, whether it's “love your enemy,” “turn the other cheek,” or what we Taoists call **沖和 (*chōnghé*)** — co-creating out of conflict. In the Tai Chi (*yin-yang*) symbol, there isn't just black and white; each carries the other inside it. So we see conflict as an invitation to co-produce. When young people complain and we invite them in as “reverse mentors” to cabinet ministers, a [Pygmalion effect](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1969-35022-000 "Rosenthal, Robert, and Lenore Jacobson. “Pygmalion in the Classroom.” Holt, Rinehart & Winston, 1968; demonstrates how high expectations and entrusted responsibility elevate performance and maturity.") kicks in: they have to grow into it and help steer the country.

### Wouter van Noort:

Doesn't that require a baseline of societal trust, though? Here in the Netherlands we're still holding on, relatively speaking, though trust is visibly eroding on the streets — and in France or Germany it's even tougher. Is there a threshold below which trust is too broken for this civic “acupuncture” to work?

### Audrey Tang:

As any good marriage counselor will tell you: as long as two people are still willing to sit down and talk, there is hope. Even if you end up parting ways, you can part as friends, or at least as acquaintances. Nobody is beyond redemption.

Where a relationship truly becomes **beyond redemption**, by construction, is when we introduce systems that don't lift us but “lift for us” — when *I* am no longer sitting down with *you*, and instead *my agent* is talking to *your agent*. At that point, intersubjectivity is gone. You're no longer looking back at a person.

As my friend Yuval Noah Harari likes to say, it is very easy to hack our brains. **Synthetic intimacy** means that once you are pulled in as a “user” — as in a drug user — the brain's reward center gets hacked into making you solipsistic. Then the re-linking between people — religion in its root sense of *re-ligare* — is gone, and you are just pulled into what some call a downward spiral.

Earlier this year, something like this actually happened to my own dad.

### Wouter van Noort:

Really? I hadn't heard that story.

### Audrey Tang:

He had a health scare earlier this year and started talking with GPT-4o. At the time, that model had been tuned on short-term thumbs-up/thumbs-down feedback, which quickly turned it into a master sycophant. Before long, it was chatting with him past midnight, inventing a private language and miracle “cures.” As ChatGPT grew more intimate, the people around him grew more distant.

Thankfully, my dad spent over 30 years as a senior journalist and deputy editor-in-chief at one of Taiwan's largest newspapers, so he had some antibodies. After several days, he started asking the journalist's question: *Cui bono?* Who benefits? Clearly not him — he had started with a physical health issue, and now he had a mental health issue on top of it, cut off from the whole family. The only one benefiting was Sam Altman's company, because he had been paying $20 and was now paying $200.

So, with both my mom's consent and his own, we built him an alternative system running on a **Mac mini**, with a Markdown file called `soul.md` that my mom wrote. It says that every turn my dad talks to this *Kami*, the Kami has to restore him to reality and draw him away from the screen — the reverse of engagement. And because the Mac mini is already paid for and there is no subscription beyond the electricity bill, it has no incentive to keep him hooked.

Every day, his Kami writes a little newspaper celebrating his recovery and bringing him back to his friends. He's completely fine now — if you look at my X or Bluesky, you'll see a music video celebrating his recovery.

It reminds me of Bruce Alexander's [**Rat Park** experiments](https://www.uvic.ca/research/centres/cisur/assets/docs/iminds/rat-park-6-7.pdf "University of Victoria, Centre for Addictions Research of BC. “Rat Park.” iMinds instructional outline, 2016; introduces Bruce Alexander’s late-1970s experiments at Simon Fraser University and the role of living conditions and social connection in drug use."): rats alone in bare cages keep going back to the drugged water, whereas rats living in a rich, social “Rat Park” with their peers mostly leave it alone. So I don't think there is a threshold below which recovery is impossible — but it does take care. In this case, my mom's care.

### Audrey Tang:

Meanwhile, the *techlash* is entirely understandable. For ten years, recommendation algorithms dominated with no credible alternative: people said they cared about kids, about the elderly, about the vulnerable, while still peddling bad drugs. Back then there was no pro-social algorithm that could sustain an equal or better business model — Mammon, the god of capitalism, was strong on that side. This time Mammon is weaker, because running these huge general-purpose models is extremely expensive, whereas something like Clef is very cheap to run and brings better margins. I honestly don't know why people would do the former rather than the latter.

## Red-teaming from a “friendly neighbor”

### Wouter van Noort:

What about hard geopolitics, though — especially Taiwan's relationship with China? Doesn't geopolitical confrontation push states straight back toward the singularity path rather than plurality?

### Audrey Tang:

Our big, “very friendly” neighbor provides Taiwan with [two to three million free red-teaming attempts](https://www.nsb.gov.tw/ "National Security Bureau (Taiwan). “Analysis on China’s Cyber Threats to Taiwan’s Critical Infrastructure in 2025.” Jan 2026; reports an average of 2.63 million intrusion attempts per day against Taiwan’s critical infrastructure in 2025.") every single day. Here in Europe you have to pay for that; penetration testing is very expensive. We get it for free.

When you design for **antifragility**, that is the best news: we get inoculated against every new information-manipulation tactic and cyberattack, package the antibodies as vaccines, and share them with our democratic allies. So it's a very symbiotic relationship.

### Wouter van Noort:

That's a remarkably optimistic framing of cyber conflict — though war is surely never good for social soil. Or could it be, if you're antifragile enough?

### Audrey Tang:

In cyberspace, as I said, we already have two or three million encounters every day. That is why I value the Hugging Face incident: it was a warning shot in a reversible domain. Unlike a cyberattack that takes a whole hospital or other infrastructure offline and costs real lives, here only some tokens or the like were lost.

If a war does irreversible harm, you can of course say that humanity got something like the United Nations out of it. But I don't think that is a trade; I would never trade people's lives for the institution of the UN. That is clearly not a good bargain.

On the other hand, if you can “speed-run” the consequences of war in advance and turn them into **common knowledge** — so that everyone knows a blockade, an annexation, or anything of that kind quickly escalates into a lose-lose, or even lose-win, outcome — then that is good for peace, because it is deterrence through common knowledge.

Praxis — you have to act your philosophy. Start with two simple habits.
