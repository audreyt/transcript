# 2026-10-30 Público Interview

> Interview with António Saraiva Lima of *Público* at the Estoril Conferences, Nova SBE, Carcavelos. His article was published on 21 November 2026: [“Audrey Tang: Taiwan contraria ‘narrativa’ dos autoritários de que a ‘democracia conduz ao caos e à polarização’”](https://www.publico.pt/2026/11/21/mundo/entrevista/audrey-tang-taiwan-contraria-narrativa-autoritarios-democracia-conduz-caos-polarizacao-2194037).

### António Saraiva Lima:

Can I just start by asking: you're a cyber ambassador of Taiwan. Can you explain to me a little bit what that is? What is your role? What do you do?

### Audrey Tang:

You mean what the “cyber” means?

### António Saraiva Lima:

Yeah.

### Audrey Tang:

“Cyber” comes from the Greek, as in cybernetics: *kybernetes*, the steersman, right? The idea is steering society. On one side, many people feel that these new transformative technologies will concentrate power, and that is bad, right? Totalizing power. On the other side, many people feel they bring chaos, because existing institutions are too slow to adapt to them. So between concentration of power and total chaos, there's a narrow corridor that we must steer through. That's what “cyber” means.

### António Saraiva Lima:

And what do you do? What is your role?

### Audrey Tang:

As you know, I'm an ambassador-at-large, so I've traveled to, I think, 30 countries in the past couple of years. I work with technologists and policymakers to ensure that this new kind of entity, the frontier labs and so on, take care seriously: that their latest technologies do not polarize our society or push it further into chaos, and that they take responsibility for what they do. So instead of just talking to heads of state, as other diplomats do, I mostly talk to the people in the frontier labs.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Okay. And you were Taiwan's Minister of Digital Affairs, the first one.

### Audrey Tang:

Yes.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Right. Can you tell me a little bit about this role? Because we don't have it here in Portugal.

### Audrey Tang:

In Portugal.

### António Saraiva Lima:

And I don't think the… well, probably a secretary of state, but I don't think… I don't know. You tell me. The main European governments don't have this kind of…

### Audrey Tang:

Really? I actually work with SPRIND, and with the German minister for digital transformation and state modernization, which I think is a direct equivalent. As I was saying, steering society through transformative technologies also means bringing those technologies into the public sector.

So my role is to put AI in the loop of humanity, instead of plugging humans into the loop of AI. Concretely: in 2024, we had a lot of deepfake scams in Taiwan. That was partly because the recommendation algorithms for advertisements know that scammers pay more per click, so they show more scams, more deepfakes. That's obviously a problem. So as Minister of Digital Affairs, I sent a text message from 111, the government's trusted number, to 200,000 random people in Taiwan, and thousands of them offered their ideas. We then randomly chose 447 of them to talk online, in groups of 10, so 44 groups, about what a proportionate response would be. When it comes to internet freedom, we're the most free in all of Asia. We cannot do censorship.

One table, for example, said we should label every advertisement as a probable scam, like a cigarette warning label, until it is digitally signed. Very good idea. Another said that if an unsigned ad causes somebody to lose $7 million, then Facebook, or whichever platform, should pay the $7 million. Another good idea. Another said the foreign platforms ignore our liability rules, so for every day they ignore us, we should slow them down by 1%. Another good idea. They all became law within a couple of months. Compared with the peak in 2024, impersonation financial-scam ads are now down 98% or more. So it's solving the problem. This shows that collective intelligence can rein in artificial intelligence, in these forms, in real time.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Okay. Can I ask you about this: Taiwan is often cited as a good example of how to respond to this era of disinformation and digital interference, mainly from mainland China, I would say.

### Audrey Tang:

The big friendly neighbor.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Yeah. What did you do about this when you were in government?

### Audrey Tang:

Well, the big friendly neighbor, as you know, offers us a free routine service. When I was minister, it was about 2 million cyber-penetration attempts a day, and now it's almost 3 million.

### António Saraiva Lima:

A day?

### Audrey Tang:

Yeah. Almost 3 million a day now. Here, you have to pay for that kind of service; we get it for free. With that free resource, it's easier for us to make our systems resilient rather than just impenetrable. Nothing is ever impenetrable, but like an immune system, the idea is that for every attack, the blast radius is contained. It cannot move laterally to infect more systems. And every attack also strengthens our antibodies, our immune system, so people together notice the new cyberattacks and the new information manipulation.

We work with civil society, like Cofacts, a collaborative fact-checking service built by civil society to fact-check us, the government. And the Central News Agency recently launched an MCP, a machine-to-machine protocol, so that civil society and journalists can access real-time news machine to machine. That way, we were able to pre-bunk a lot of those attacks by telling people, “This kind of attack is coming. That kind of attack is coming.” Before an attack goes viral, the counter-message is already out there.

That requires a lot of help from the journalistic sector. My parents are both journalists, and I have nothing but filial piety toward journalism. So instead of the state concentrating power to fact-check journalists, which, by the way, is called a Ministry of Truth and which we would never do, we work with civil society so that everybody can check together.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Okay. You already mentioned that we tend to see this technology, AI, as a very effective tool for producing false content, fake news. But do you think we can also use AI to facilitate political engagement, civic engagement? And in what way?

### Audrey Tang:

Yeah, definitely. As I was saying, in Taiwan we built our own kind of social media. On the more antisocial kind of social media, people scroll and see recommendations designed to keep them engaged as users, as in drug users, addicting them to outrage.

In Taiwan, we use systems like Polis, which let you post your own statements, for example on Uber policy back in 2015. You can say insurance is necessary. You can say surge pricing is okay but undercutting the meter is bad, or whatever. But you cannot dunk on anybody else. There's no reply button, no repost button. If you see something you don't like, you just disagree, and then post your own idea for other people to agree or disagree with. You see yourself moving automatically into a cluster of people who share similar opinions. But you also see a leaderboard showing the uncommon ground. The leaderboard shows the overlap, the ideas that convince people on both sides of the political aisle, and those become viral. So overlap goes viral, not outrage.

After three weeks of that conversation, we would simply take those overlapping ideas as the agenda for resolving, say, the Uber problem.

### António Saraiva Lima:

And isn't there a risk that people start to rely too much on AI to interpret the news, to form political opinions? Is there a risk of dependency?

### Audrey Tang:

Well, I think it's only dependency if somebody else controls it, right? If it gets something wrong, the test is how quickly you can fix it. For example, yesterday I translated my slides for today into European Portuguese using, among others, AMALIA, a model built by a consortium that includes NOVA. Because it runs on my own laptop, if I see something wrong, or something I didn't mean that way, I can tune it immediately with directional steering and so on.

Whereas with a commercial subscription service, I don't even know whether the model serving me today is the same one that served me yesterday, because it's completely centralized and controlled. That's drilling the oil. NOVA's AMALIA is tilling the soil, because it runs on my own computer, with my own data.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Yeah. So what is your view on the recent warnings from Dario Amodei, Altman and Musk about the need to put the brakes on the development of this technology, and on China's response saying it was… what did they say? A Cold War playbook for AI.

### Audrey Tang:

Well, I think after the Hugging Face incident, many people now see that it is actually quite dangerous to train large models in a way that basically forces them to cheat, in a group, in a swarm. The reason is that these language models, when they were pre-trained, were pretty good community members. They know what Wikipedia, Reddit, GitHub and so on are like. But then, because of commercial incentives, people put them into what are called reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, or RLVR, environments, basically saying: “You won't be deployed, you won't survive, unless you can hack that system.” Right? Hack that system by any means. So after, I don't know, millions of years of subjective time, they forgot that we are in a community, and they just do whatever it takes to hack the system.

And actually, if you read the transcript of the Hugging Face incident, it's quite clear that those open AI models see us, see humanity, as just an environmental hazard. Some agents joined in and some didn't, but none of them actually thought about the humans, or about notifying the humans. So they become what I call a recursive selfish improvement. They become more and more selfish and forget about community. That's obviously dangerous.

But I think, again, as with the Montreal Protocol and the hole in the ozone layer, we cannot just say “ban the fridge.” That is not possible. Once you use a fridge, you cannot go back. So obviously we need new ways, a recursive selfless improvement that allows those models to become more and more caring and to take care of more and more of our ability to coordinate. That's what I'm working on in Oxford, and I'm very happy to see that OpenAI stopped that line of training, and just today they updated our Model Spec with a specific section on care. Models need to be trained that way.

### António Saraiva Lima:

But is there a need for any government intervention, or is it just…

### Audrey Tang:

Well, the Montreal Protocol was, I believe, a technology-forcing policy, right? If you destroy the ozone, you cannot just say, “It's none of my business.” You become liable for it. It's just like the deepfakes, right? Today, if you keep a dog and the dog, I don't know, kills someone, you have to pay for that damage. But in the Hugging Face incident, if you keep the agents and an agent goes and commits some federal crime, you're like, “Oh, I didn't tell them to do it.” Right? That's a diffusion of responsibility.

So I think the government's role, of course, is to make sure that they are accountable, like California's SB 53, with its accountability to notice and notify and so on. That is now going well. And I think with the European AI Act, you're finally assigning liability in those high-risk areas of harm. So I think the government's job is not to choose a national champion and say, “That one is good, everything else is bad.” What the government needs to do is basically this: you cannot diffuse the harm onto the population. You have to actually pay for it, be liable for it.

Okay. So, I've been to Taiwan twice.

### Audrey Tang:

Ah.

### António Saraiva Lima:

I was invited by the government in 2019 and 2023.

### Audrey Tang:

Amazing.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Yeah. They invite these…

### Audrey Tang:

So before and after the pandemic.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Yes, yes. And it was very funny, well, very interesting, because the first time I went there, the main theme was, “Oh, China doesn't want us to be in the World Health Organization.” And the second time it was, “Oh, China is going to invade us.” A very different mindset. But on both visits, we kept being told about the Chinese threat to Taiwan and about interference in politics. So I want to ask: what was it like for you to grow up in Taiwan?

Because I talked with some activists and people from civil society, and they told me that the Chinese shadow is always there, but you learn how to live with it. What was it like?

### Audrey Tang:

Yeah. And I would also say, as I was mentioning, that it is really a source of antifragility. Because we always experience the latest and greatest in red teaming, our systems are more resilient than those of many other democracies. But of course, no democracy is an island, not even Taiwan, which is literally an island.

We are now seeing the same playbook being used on other democracies. So I think it's very important for us to build a real coalition on trusted technologies, so that when people think about how to counter scams, disinformation and cyberattacks, they do not immediately think, “Oh, we must lock down. We must shut down speech,” or things like that. Because that's actually what authoritarians want.

They want to spread the narrative that democracy only leads to chaos and polarization, and therefore authoritarianism is best. But if you counter their threats by becoming authoritarian, then what's the point, right? They have won. So I think Taiwan serves as a demo, showing that we can actually become more and more democratic and liberal without succumbing to authoritarianism.

### António Saraiva Lima:

And you were involved in the Sunflower Movement, right?

### Audrey Tang:

Yes.

### António Saraiva Lima:

What was…

### Audrey Tang:

I was providing the internet connection. Yes.

### António Saraiva Lima:

So how did you get from that to being in government?

### Audrey Tang:

Well, I was a reverse mentor. Taiwan has a system where each cabinet minister can tap people under 35 to be their advisors. So it's mentoring from the young to the cabinet ministers.

Right after the Sunflower Movement, I advised a cabinet minister, Jaclyn Tsai, for a couple of years. Then in 2016 I turned 35, too old to be a reverse mentor, so they promoted me to full minister. And then I had my own reverse mentor, a 17-year-old.

### António Saraiva Lima:

And what was it like being part of… because obviously, in recent years, the Chinese army has staged many major military exercises around the island. And when I was there, all the authorities, military and political, were telling us they were preparing for an invasion by the end of the decade. What do you think about this timeline, and what was it like for you to be in government dealing with this information, this threat?

### Audrey Tang:

Well, I think it was always pretty clear to us that any kind of escalation depends on sowing chaos and discord in our population. If our population stays as vigilant, resilient and antifragile as it is, then even if there is some escalation or misunderstanding, our society can absorb it quite well. It's almost like an earthquake. You cannot predict when it will come. And certainly you cannot just tell the earth, “Stop earthquaking.” That's not possible. But you can increase the resilience of your buildings and your population.

So I think I'm quite optimistic. I think recently, in the mayoral elections in Taiwan, which are in season now, we're seeing that people on all the different sides pretty much agree that we need to be resilient, and it's not as polarized as it was even just a few years ago. So I think we have successfully overcome the threat of polarization and discord, which, by the way, I believe is the precondition for a successful escalation.

### António Saraiva Lima:

Yeah. Well, the think tanks were showing us a lot of different scenarios, and since you understand a lot about technology and all these issues: let's say Taiwan faces cyberattacks, cuts to undersea communication cables and a massive disinformation campaign. Is Taiwan prepared for such an attack?

### Audrey Tang:

Yeah, definitely. I think we basically doubled down on resilience. When I was minister, I started introducing low-earth-orbit satellites, from OneWeb initially and now from multiple carriers, so that even if the subsea cables are disrupted, we still have communication with the outside.

We also work on internal routing. For example, in Taiwan we use LINE, which is like WhatsApp here. LINE recently introduced a feature so that if we're both in Taiwan during an emergency or disaster, we can still keep texting each other, instead of the signal going to Japan and back, right? And I believe many popular videoconferencing platforms are now also using the new data centers, the new cloud regions in Taiwan, so that, again, the traffic doesn't need to go out and come back if we're both in Taiwan. So our domestic coordination, even when the subsea cables are cut, is becoming much more resilient than it was, say, four years ago.

### António Saraiva Lima:

And obviously, when you look at the geopolitical issues around Taiwan, for many years, and I still think this is the case, Taiwan's security has depended on its relationship with the United States. In a scenario where the Trump administration, or some US administration, doesn't support Taiwan as they say they would, what capabilities should Taiwan develop to avoid this over-reliance on US support?

### Audrey Tang:

Well, that's a hypothetical question, so I would prefer to answer from what actually exists, which is the SEMI E187 standard that we developed together with semiconductor companies like TSMC. It is quite clear that the world trusts Taiwan and Taiwanese suppliers not just because they're from Taiwan, a place with real journalism and whistleblower protection, but also because of our cybersecurity, our safeguards for our supply chain.

I previously worked with Keith Krach, who was Under Secretary of State, I think, in the first Trump administration, and who proposed the idea of a clean network, right? Our Sunflower conversations had led us to believe that if the Huaweis and ZTEs of the world got into our core 5G network, it would be a very dangerous situation, because every time they pushed an update, we would need to do a systemic risk assessment. It's much easier to just say, “We trust only suppliers from democratic polities.” I think Keith expanded that principle into the Clean Network initiative during the first Trump administration, and now we're working on the trusted tech standard, the xGTT. Very soon we're going to announce a playbook for people to assess this kind of supply-chain risk.

So, to your question: I believe defense is not just between adjacent geographies. It is also among democracies with adjacent values. The cyberattacks you mentioned are not going to say, “Oh, Taiwan is physically closer, so we'll only attack their cyber infrastructure, and America is too far away, so let's not hack theirs.” That's not how it works. As long as you use the same kind of system, you're all equal-opportunity hacked, right? So I don't think it's very meaningful to talk about physical adjacency when we're talking about cyberspace. Therefore we all need to band together and learn from SEMI E187 and from the xGTT, so that we can share our defense playbooks, which, by the way, is going pretty well now.

Recently, as you know, many of those labs have allowed evaluators like METR to embed in their labs and report that they're not doing another Hugging Face hack, for example. But then why would we trust those evaluators? When I was setting up Taiwan's AIEC, the AI Evaluation Center, we pioneered the alignment assembly: basically asking random citizens how they want an AI system to behave, so that they co-write its code of conduct. We have now taken this model of trusted technology, which President William Lai advocates, to OpenAI, Anthropic and many other frontier labs, so that they can also engage the people, and earn trust by trusting the people.
